# Chapter 12: The Threshold of the Iron Vanguard

The central coordination desks were half-buried under stacks of damp parchment and red cinnabar ink-pads.

Chaitanya and Anil stepped up the granite stairs of the eastern terrace, merging into the slow, subdued stream of certified students. Across the lower terraces, the contrast was sharp and painful: long files of rejected novices were already hauling their cedar baggage chests toward the southern service gates, their heads down, escorted by unyielding squads of imperial spearmen.

Near the base of the central colonnade, Bennett was already waiting. 

His pale blue coat was neatly buttoned, though a spray of frozen droplets still glistened along his left sleeve. Beside him stood Tejaswini, her arms crossed over the crimson breastplate of her light armor, leaning her shoulder against a fluted pillar. Her leather gloves were tucked into her belt; her dark hair was gathered into a practical knot at the nape of her neck.

As Chaitanya approached, her dark eyes swept him from brow to boots, noting the clean weave of his grey tunic and the absence of torn fabric. A quiet, guarded breath left her lips.

"You took your time," she said softly, though the slight tilt of her chin betrayed her relief.

"Candidate Ninety," Chaitanya replied, offering a faint, self-deprecating shrug as he tapped his stamped vellum against his knuckles. "Rann doesn't hurry his ledger."

Anil, despite his torn trouser leg and bruised shoulder, beamed like a boy who had survived a river flood. "You should have seen him, Tejaswini! The blades stopped four feet out. Dead in the air. Rann looked like he’d swallowed an entire copper coin."

Bennett adjusted the silver-blue sash at his waist, a dry smirk touching his aristocratic lips. "If you made Proctor Rann lose his composure, Chaitanya, you may find your name entered into the disciplinary files before nightfall. That man considers a pulse to be an act of insubordination."

"How were the cisterns?" Chaitanya asked, turning toward Bennett.

"Methodical," Bennett answered quietly. "The water temperature was near freezing. They required three cycles of structural freezing and rapid thawing without shattering the bronze basin. Master Cole gave his mark without comment."

Tejaswini tapped the iron rail with her knuckle. "Vane had six of us in the stone amphitheater. Full-arc thermal barrages. Two students melted their practice targets and scorched the proctor's boot. He cleared three of us and sent the others to the border logistics draft."

She looked out across the sea of students filling the grand courtyard below.

Over three thousand young men and women were gathered in disciplined formation between the grey statues of the Founding Chancellors. The bronze muster bells, usually rung to signal evening study hall, were tolling with a slow, heavy resonance that echoed off the granite cliffs.

Then, from the high central tower, the iron gates of the Grand Balcony groaned inward.

The murmuring across the parade grounds died in a fraction of a second.

High Chancellor Soman stepped out onto the granite parapet.

He was a man carved from the same hard grey stone as the academy walls. His hair was cropped close, iron-grey at the temples, and over his heavy wool magistrate’s coat rested an archaic breastplate of blackened steel, unadorned save for the three-fold sun of the unified empire. On either side of him stood two imperial commanders in full combat plate, their hands resting on the pommels of heavy broadswords.

Soman placed his leather-gloved hands upon the parapet. He did not use an iron speaking trumpet. His voice, amplified by subtle acoustic resonators built into the masonry of the central facade, rolled down across the three thousand upturned faces with the dry, crushing weight of an avalanche.

"Students of Tattva," the Chancellor began, his words deliberate and unhurried. "For one hundred and ninety years, this institution has operated under the charter of the Last Emperor. We were founded to catalog the laws of nature, to cultivate the inherited gifts of the four elements, and to instruct the youth of this province in the arts of peace, administration, and civic order."

He paused, his cold, pale eyes sweeping the quiet ranks below.

"That charter is dissolved."

A sharp, collective intake of air hissed through the courtyard. 

Chaitanya did not move. Beside him, he heard Anil’s jaw click shut, and Tejaswini’s hand shifted to rest instinctively against the hilt of her hunting knife.

"The sky has broken our peace," Soman continued, his tone devoid of regret. "The arrival of the second celestial body during the Solstice has altered the planetary balance permanently. Across our borders, ancient boundaries are collapsing. Tribes and raiders in the northern wastes have awakened to martial power; the beasts of the Deadwinter fault are stirring in numbers not recorded since the First Dynasty. The luxury of theoretical scholarship is an indulgence the empire can no longer afford."

Soman leaned forward, his black iron gorget catching the dull red light of the setting sun.

"As of the fourth bell of this evening, Tattva Academy ceases to exist as a collegiate school. By imperial decree, this fortress is redesignated as the First Northern Vanguard Garrison. Every person standing upon these stones has demonstrated functional combat bending; every person who could not has been reassigned to imperial infrastructure."

The words fell like iron weights onto the frozen gravel.

"There will be no examinations in philosophy. There will be no degrees in natural history. Starting tomorrow, all certified disciples will be organized into four-person expeditionary units. You will receive tactical instruction in monster suppression, frontier coordination, and high-intensity elemental warfare. By the turn of the spring thaw, your units will be deployed into the border provinces."

Soman straightened his spine, his cold gaze resting upon the silent assembly.

"The world that sheltered your childhood is dead. You are no longer disciples seeking understanding. You are the shield of this empire, or you are its casualties. Dismissed."

The Chancellor did not wait for applause. He did not wait for a salute.

He turned on his heel, his heavy wool cloak sweeping behind him, and stepped back through the iron doors into the tower. The two armoured commanders followed, and the heavy gates clanged shut with a final, hollow *boom* that vibrated through the flagstones beneath Chaitanya’s boots.

The space upon the high balcony was suddenly empty.

It was an emptiness that did not heal. It hung above the parade grounds like an open wound, cold, final, and absolute.

Down in the courtyard, no one cheered. No one wept. 

Three thousand students stood in the gathering dark, their breath pluming white against the freezing sky, looking at one another with the dazed, terrified realization that their lives had been rewritten in five minutes.

Bennett was the first to speak. His voice was low, taut, and razor-sharp.

"Four-person expeditionary units," Bennett murmured, looking at the three of them.

Anil looked from Bennett to Tejaswini, then to Chaitanya. "We already live in the same room."

Tejaswini’s chin lifted, her crimson mantle catching the mountain wind. "Then we don't look for anyone else. We register our unit first thing in the morning."

Chaitanya looked up at the high stone balcony, then down at the plain iron band on his wrist.

The empire had drawn its line in the dirt. But as the dark settled over the frozen peaks, Chaitanya knew with quiet certainty that the storm coming to this world was far larger than the Chancellor’s iron breastplate—and that their survival would depend on the secrets buried beneath the academy's oldest stones.
