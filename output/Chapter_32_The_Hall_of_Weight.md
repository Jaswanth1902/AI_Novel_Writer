# Chapter 32: The Hall of Weight

The corridor beyond the infirmary felt longer than it ever had.

Its barrel-vaulted ceiling of grey limestone rose twenty cubits above the floor, the arches supported by massive square columns carved with the names of forgotten frontier regiments. The stones were cold, sweating mineral moisture that dripped into the drainage gutters with the steady cadence of a water clock.

Bennett walked at the front.

Command had always seemed straightforward in the training manuals: maintain formation, enforce supply discipline, deploy the elements according to the Fourfold Inscription. But out on the cobblestones of Langford, command had turned into the sound of his own ribs splintering against an ice wall, the sight of Anil buried under stone eaves, and the image of Chaitanya’s broken body lying in a bin of white flour.

A commander did not simply direct blows; a commander carried the blood of his comrades on his knuckles.

Anil walked three paces behind, his steps measured and quiet. He did not look at the tapestries or the iron weapon racks along the walls. His eyes remained fixed on the back of Bennett’s wool coat, matching his stride to the rhythmic thud of the pine crutch.

At the end of the north gallery, an orderly in an iron breastplate intercepted them, presenting a wooden token stamped with a crossed hammer and pick.

"Proctor Rao requests the squad leader in the Western Bastion," the orderly said.

They turned down the spiraling stairs that led to the artillery works.

The Western Bastion was a hollowed granite battery overlooking the valley gorge. Five massive embrasures opened through twelve-foot stone walls, each housing an iron-framed heavy ballista capable of hurling four-inch steel bolts across the river. The air smelled of graphite grease, raw coal smoke, and the sharp tang of iron filings.

Proctor Rao stood beside a dismounted ballista carriage, a heavy iron wrench in his right hand. He wore a stained leather work apron over his grey tunic, his massive forearms blackened with graphite to the elbows.

He turned as Bennett’s crutch scraped on the flagstones.

"Sit," Rao growled, pointing the wrench toward a pair of low oak benches beside an iron brazier.

Bennett and Anil sat down. The heat from the glowing coals struck Bennett’s bruised ribs with a comforting, dull warmth that eased the constant spasm in his side.

Rao wiped his hands on a greasy hemp rag, tossed the wrench onto an iron anvil, and stood over them, his broad frame blocking the light from the embrasure.

"The physician’s report came across my desk an hour ago," Rao said. His voice was quiet, stripped of the booming theatricality he used on the drill field. "Chaitanya’s left clavicle is crushed. Three ribs fractured. Internal conduits running hot enough to curdle tallow. By all rights of nature, that boy should have arrived at the junction in a pine box."

"He held the line, Proctor," Bennett said, holding the earth-master’s heavy gaze without flinching.

"I know he held the line," Rao countered, his jaw clenching until the scar on his temple turned white. "And so does the Ministry of Inscription. They have three scribes in the Records Office right now demanding to inspect the carcass of that Danava. They want to know how four candidate cubs turned an apex siege-beast inside out."

Anil’s hands tightened on his knees. "Lyra’s dispatch stated that we used sulfur charges."

Rao let out a harsh, dry breath through his nostrils. "Lyra is a clever scout, boy. Her dispatch will hold the magistrates at bay for ten days. But paper does not stop preceptors who smell heresy. The Ministry believes Chaitanya used an unapproved artifact, or worse: that he broke the Four Gates."

He reached to the bench, picked up a rolled parchment stamped with the golden seal of the Grand Preceptor, and tossed it into Bennett’s lap.

Bennett unrolled the skin.

*Imperial Order of Vanguard Selection.*  
*Pursuant to the Southern Defense Decree, all Candidate Vanguard Units will muster for the Annual Selection Tournament at the beginning of the coming month. The evaluation will be conducted under open elimination brackets in the Grand Arena of Sand and Iron. The top-ranking unit will receive immediate field commissions and full operational autonomy under the Frontier High Command.*

Bennett looked up, his brow furrowed. "The tournament was scheduled for late spring."

"The spring thaw is coming early," Rao said, stepping to the open embrasure and looking out over the misty mountain passes. "The scouts in the southern trenches report that the sinkholes are boiling with miasma. The Danava you killed in Langford was not an invasion force, Bennett. It was an outrunner: an alpha driven out of the high peaks by something worse."

A cold draft swept through the embrasure, stirring the ashes in the brazier.

"The frontier needs commanders who can kill monsters," Rao said, turning back to face them. "If you win that tournament outright, the Commandant signs your field commissions. Once you are commissioned officers of the Frontier Line, the Inscription magistrates cannot touch you without an imperial warrant signed by the Emperor himself."

Bennett gripped the parchment tightly, the wax crumbling between his fingers.

"And if we lose?"

Rao’s grey eyes were hard as granite.

"If you lose, Unit Seven is disbanded. Chaitanya goes to the Ministry’s testing chambers in the capital, Lady Varma is returned to her clan under armed guard, and you spend the rest of your youth digging latrine ditches along the Breach."

Rao leaned down, resting his massive, grease-stained palm on Bennett’s uninjured shoulder.

"Tell the noble girl to keep him breathing, Bennett," Rao whispered. "And when Chaitanya opens his eyes, you get him onto the sand. Because the sand is the only place left where you can fight for your lives."
