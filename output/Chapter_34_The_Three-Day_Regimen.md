# Chapter 34: The Three-Day Regimen

The rhythm held.

For three days, the cadence of the Academy did not alter by a single heartbeat. The candidates trained; they rested; they trained again. No speeches were delivered from the high balconies. No comparisons were drawn between squads. Each unit followed the same brutal regimen of long hours, controlled strain, and measured physical recovery. Muscles tore and re-knitted thicker; palms calloused over blood blisters; minds learned to halt before permanent structural damage and resume before comfort could soften the bone.

In the shared quarters of Unit 07, Bennett enforced the discipline of a northern garrison sergeant.

Every evening at fifth watch, an iron kettle of boiled marrow broth simmered on the corner stove, seasoned with coarse rock salt and dried mountain thyme. Bennett ladled the broth into four wooden bowls, insisting that Anil and Tejaswini drink down every drop to replace the calcium and iron burned away on the sand rings. His own cracked ribs were stabilizing under the linen wraps, the pain turning from a sharp, breath-stealing spike into a dull, grinding ache that allowed him to walk without the crutch.

Anil spent his rest hours at the narrow writing desk beneath the window, a pile of unbound candidate registers spread before him by the light of a tallow dip.

"Unit One," Anil said on the third evening, tapping the rough rag paper with a charcoal pencil. "Anirudh of the Capital Prefecture. Third-tier senior squad. Two lightning specialists, an iron-shaper, and an offensive water bender. They took the spring honors last year with twenty-seven unbroken circle victories."

Bennett swallowed his broth slowly, his throat working. "Anirudh fights with twin short swords, doesn't he?"

"Broad-bladed curved sabres," Anil corrected, looking up. "He channels the charge along the spine of the steel, discharging on impact rather than throwing distance bolts like Proctor Meera. He doesn't waste energy on spectacular arcs; he cuts to sever tendons. His squad is already calling themselves the Imperial Vanguard."

"Let them call themselves whatever pleases their vanity," Bennett grunted, setting his bowl on the table. "On the sand, vanity does not stop a six-foot spear."

Across the quadrangle, in the quiet vault of the inner infirmary, the Chief Physician cut the linen bindings from Chaitanya’s left shoulder with a pair of curved bronze shears.

The physician was an old man named Vasishta, whose spectacles were bound with copper wire and whose fingers smelled of camphor and dried leeches. He laid the soiled vinegar dressings in a wooden basin, then reached out with two calloused thumbs to probe the alignment of Chaitanya’s left clavicle.

Tejaswini sat on the corner stool, her back straight, her dark eyes tracking every movement of the old man’s hands.

Vasishta tapped the bone gently with a miniature bronze mallet. The sound was crisp, dull, and solid: the acoustic resonance of unbroken granite.

The old man frowned, pushing his spectacles up the bridge of his nose. He pressed his fingers deep into the muscle beneath the collarbone, feeling for the step-fracture that had separated the bone forty-eight hours earlier. There was no step. There was no jagged ridge of provisional callus. The bone was smooth, aligned to the millimeter, its internal density matching that of an adult warrior who had never suffered a fracture in his life.

"This is impossible," Vasishta muttered to himself, shaking his grey head. "A three-point fracture of the clavicle requires six weeks in cedar splints to achieve fibrous union. The marrow here is completely restored. It feels... as though the bone was forged in an iron furnace rather than knitted by blood."

He turned to Tejaswini, his brow furrowed with deep professional suspicion. "Did your clan provide him with southern alchemical tinctures, Lady Varma? Imperial elixir pills?"

"My family provides weapons, not medicine, Physician," Tejaswini replied, her voice cool and unhurried as she leaned back against the stone wall. "Chaitanya has worked the mountain farms since childhood. His constitution has always withstood what would break softer men."

Vasishta grumbled under his breath, wiping his bronze mallet on his apron. "Softer men or not, the human body does not ignore the laws of anatomy. If he were a horse, I would say he was blessed by the mountain spirits."

He collected his instruments into his leather roll, picked up the basin of soiled dressings, and shuffled toward the door. "Remove the splints tomorrow morning. If he wakes, give him weak barley water. No salt meat until the stomach settles."

The heavy oak door closed behind him, the iron latch dropping with a muffled clatter.

Silence returned to the vaulted room.

Outside, the rain had ceased, leaving the night sky clear and cold. A full moon climbed above the eastern battlements, casting long, rectangular bars of silver light through the high slit-window. The pale illumination fell across the flagstones, creeping up the edge of the mattress until it touched Chaitanya’s resting hands.

Tejaswini rose from her stool. She took a clean linen cloth, dipped it into the earthenware basin of cold well-water, and gently wiped the traces of camphor ointment from his collarbone and throat.

The skin beneath her fingers was warm and firm. Beneath the pale skin of his chest, the rhythmic rise and fall of his lungs had shifted: deeper, more deliberate, locking into the four-beat diaphragmatic cadence of his waking meditation.

She stopped. Her hand hovered an inch above his heart.

On the rough grey blanket, Chaitanya’s right fingers moved.

His knuckles contracted once, twice, testing the friction of the wool fibers between his fingertips. A short, quiet breath hissed inward through his teeth.

Then, slowly, his eyelids drew back.

In the silver moonlight of the infirmary, his dark eyes opened: clear, unclouded by fever or pain, reflecting the quiet stars beyond the slit-window with the terrifying, unbroken stillness of a deep mountain lake.
