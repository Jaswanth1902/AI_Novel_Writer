# Chapter 13: The Subterranean Folio

By the eighth bell of night, the sound of iron chisels rang through the darkened corridors of Tattva Academy.

Workmen in grease-stained canvas tunics stood on wooden trestles beneath the vaulted archways of the central quadrangle, their hammers striking cold steel with rhythmic, hollow blows. One by one, the carved stone mottoes of the collegiate founders (*Through Inquiry, Harmony; Through Harmony, Wisdom*) were being sheared away, falling to the flagstones in jagged grey chips that smelled of ancient mortar and lime.

Above the doorways, new rectangular iron plates were being bolted directly into the stone: *Armory Sector Four*, *Munitions Ingestion*, *Barracks Six*.

Chaitanya kept his head low, his dark slate coat pulled close, his soft-soled felt boots making no sound against the masonry.

He avoided the central thoroughfares where imperial sentries in fluted breastplates stood at every intersection, checking identity tablets against freshly inked military registers. Instead, he slipped through the narrow drainage breezeway behind the botanical conservatories, descending the worn spiraling steps that plunged sixty feet into the bedrock foundations beneath the Great Archive.

Down here, the cold was ancient and still.

The air smelled of cedar shavings, damp river loam, and the sour animal grease of unrefined tallow lamps. Rows of heavy oak doors, their iron hinges rusted shut from decades of damp, stretched down the low-ceilinged corridor.

At the far end of Cellar Three, a thin sliver of amber light leaked beneath a warped pine lintel.

Chaitanya tapped the wood three times: two short raps, one heavy.

A bolt scraped back on its track. The door opened four inches, revealing the pinched, anxious face of Nikos. The old archivist’s round spectacles were perched on his nose with twine; his wool cardigan was dusted with chalk; his hands trembled against the door frame.

"Chaitanya," the old man whispered, his breath pluming pale in the draft. "You shouldn't be down here. The provost's guards searched the upper tiers an hour ago. They've requisitioned the cartography vaults for black-powder kegs."

"I know," Chaitanya said softly, sliding through the gap as Nikos pulled the heavy door shut and dropped the crossbar into place. "That is why I came. Soman announced the reorganization on the balcony. The academic archives are being liquidated."

Nikos sat heavily on an overturned cedar crate, pressing his ink-stained palms to his forehead. 

The small subterranean vault was a disaster of hurried desperation. Leather-bound folios were stacked in precarious towers along the limestone walls; bundles of parchment tied with rough hemp cord were stuffed into canvas grain sacks; loose sheets of sheepskin covered the single pine workbench, held down against the drafts with smooth river stones.

"Forty-two years," Nikos murmured, his voice cracked with dry grief. "I helped catalogue the First Emperor’s revenue rolls with Master Hemant when we were both twenty. Now a captain with three medals walks into the stacks, points his riding crop at five hundred years of history, and orders it crated for fire-starters at the winter forts."

Chaitanya knelt on the flagstones beside the workbench, resting a hand on the old man's knee. "They can burn the timber, Nikos. They cannot burn what we carry out. Where is the stele translation?"

Nikos looked up, his bloodshot eyes searching Chaitanya’s face.

"You know what they do to students caught with pre-imperial texts," the old man rasped. "The First Dynasty is forbidden. The Emperor unified the language for a reason: he wanted every man to believe the four elements were born from his crown. If the provost finds those pages on you, you won't be sent to the southern baggage trains. You'll simply disappear."

"The provost doesn't look at books," Chaitanya replied, his voice level, quiet, and unshakeable. "He looks at weapons. And if I don't read what's in that folio, we will spend the next three years dying on the frontier without ever understanding what we were fighting for."

Nikos stared at him for a long, silent moment. Then, with a slow, trembling sigh, the old archivist reached into his wool cardigan, pulled out a small iron pry-bar, and walked to the corner of the cellar.

He knelt beside the stone drainage trough and wedged the iron tool beneath the corner of a broad, cracked slate tile.

With a dry grunt, Nikos lifted the stone.

Beneath the floor lay a shallow depression lined with dry cedar bark. Resting inside was a thick bundle of yellowed vellum sheets, wrapped tightly in oilcloth and bound with three wraps of blackened leather cord.

Nikos lifted the package with both hands, as though he were carrying an unexploded shell, and placed it on the pine desk between them.

"The late Master Varun spent eleven years in the border monasteries copying the inscriptions from the sunken obelisk at the edge of the Deadwinter fault," Nikos said softly, his thumb tracing the oilcloth. "It is the only complete copy of the *Discourse on the Unified Root and Divine Mechanism*. When the imperial censors purged the monastery library thirty years ago, Varun carried this out beneath his tunic."

Nikos pushed the bundle across the wood.

"He died three winters ago. He told me to wait for someone who looked at the sky and asked *why*, instead of asking *how much*. You're the only one who ever came, Chaitanya."

Chaitanya reached out. 

His fingertips touched the cold, oil-slick fabric. A profound, solemn stillness settled over his ribs, silencing the distant echoes of chisels and marching boots above.

He untied the leather cord.

The oilcloth fell away, revealing thirty pages of thick, brittle vellum. The edges were uneven, dark with the grime of centuries, but the ink upon the surface was stark, black, and unyielding. The script was archaic, a dense, pre-imperial calligraphy where Sanskrit roots merged with mathematical geometry, crowded with intricate marginal notes, tangent diagrams, and concentric circular mandalas.

Chaitanya pulled a three-legged wooden stool to the desk. He trimmed the rush wick of the fat lamp, letting the small, steady amber flame illuminate the opening paragraph.

"Go back to your room before the fourth hour," Nikos whispered, walking toward the small cot in the corner of the vault. "If you take it out of this room, you carry your own execution order."

"I won't take it out," Chaitanya murmured, his eyes already locked onto the opening line of the ancient ink. "I'll keep it here."

He touched his left temple with his thumb, bent his spine over the yellowed parchment, and began to read.
