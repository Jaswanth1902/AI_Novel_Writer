## 📖 STAGE 4: THE RENDERED DEFINITIVE CHAPTER PROSE

```markdown
The joy of the night before evaporated with the morning frost.

Chaitanya woke with his cheek pressed against the cold pine planks of the floor. For several heartbeats, he did not move, letting his awareness linger in the receding memory of Anil’s ridiculous laughter, the hot cumin oil on torn flatbread, and the heavy, living warmth of Tejaswini’s forehead resting against his collarbone.

Then the silence of the room asserted itself.

It was not the warm, companionable silence of a household asleep. It was the sterile, expectant quiet of an empty hall before an inspection.

Chaitanya sat up, his stiff joints cracking in the freezing air. Down the narrow corridor, the iron spout of the washroom hissed as cold well-water splashed into the stone basin. Anil was awake. Tejaswini’s cedar door remained firmly latched, though a faint ribbon of yellow lamp-light spilled beneath the jamb.

Chaitanya stood, rolled his sleeping mat against the partition, and walked barefoot into the common room. 

He pulled out a heavy pine stool at the dining table. The surface of the wood was grey and scarred with a decade of knife marks, ink spills, and hot pot rings. He rested his elbows on the rough grain, his thumb mindlessly tracing a deep, jagged groove near the edge.

*Classes,* he thought. *I have to report to the registration desk. I have to declare.*

The thought refused to settle into satisfaction. Instead, it sank like a cold iron weight into his gut.

Why air?

In all the ancient imperial scrolls, the elements were described as expressions of the spirit. Earth belonged to the steadfast and unyielding; water answered the fluid and adaptable; fire burned within the passionate and the proud. Air was the element of detachment, of lightness, of unburdened peace.

Anil was born for it. Anil didn't have a single rancorous bone in his chest; he followed his father’s quiet philosophy without effort, breathing in calm and breathing out laughter.

Chaitanya was the opposite. His mind was a cage of crows—restless, suspicious, constantly dissecting every syllable, calculating exits, anticipating betrayals. He was heavy with unresolved questions and scarred by a childhood spent in the shadow of an absent father and an impossible sister.

If air was the art of letting go, then he was the worst candidate in the empire.

*What is the essence of bending?* he asked himself, closing his eyes against the dim grey dawn. *What did the old masters miss?*

In the darkness behind his eyelids, an image surfaced with photographic clarity.

It was not a memory of a lesson or a sparring match. It was the black-and-white emulsion plate he had exposed in the deepest sub-vault of the Grand Archive three weeks before the Solstice, using his contraband spring-aperture brass box.

On that plate was a rubbing of the pre-imperial pillar from the First Dynasty—text carved centuries before the Chakravarta unified the continent. The translation he had pieced together in secret still burned in his memory:

*The four rivers do not spring from four mountains. They are four gates carved into the same stone basin. The hand that seeks to hold the water will drown; the hand that opens the gate becomes the river.*

Chaitanya’s eyes snapped open.

"Chaitanya! Are you deaf today?"

Bennett was standing three paces away, framed by the open doorway to the corridor. His pale blue cadet coat was buttoned to the chin, but his hair was windblown, and his grey eyes were dark with an intensity Chaitanya had never seen in him before.

Chaitanya blinked, rubbing his temples. "Bennett. I was... thinking. What's happened?"

Anil emerged from the washroom, wiping his wet face with a coarse linen towel, his sleeves rolled to his elbows. At the same moment, Tejaswini’s door unlatched, and she stepped into the common room in her dark red combat trousers, her eyes darting instantly to Bennett's face.

Bennett did not sit. He walked to the table and dropped a heavy document bound with thick hemp cord and sealed with a dollop of harsh red wax.

"All standard curriculum lectures are suspended," Bennett said, his voice flat, clipped, and completely stripped of his usual dry irony. "The High Council issued an imperial reclassification directive at the third hour of the morning."

Anil lowered his towel. "Suspended? For how long?"

"Indefinitely," Bennett replied. "By noon, every student who awakened to an ability over the last forty-eight hours must report to the eastern muster grounds for mandatory practical examination. Failure to appear is classified as active desertion."

Tejaswini crossed her arms, leaning against the counter. "It’s just an intake assessment, Bennett. With hundreds of new benders appearing overnight, the proctors need to know who belongs in which cohort."

"You don't understand," Bennett said, his voice dropping into a harsh whisper as he tapped the red wax seal with the tip of his forefinger. "This isn't an intake. It's a purge."

The word dropped into the room like a dead crow.

"What are you talking about?" Anil asked, his brow furrowing.

"The council met in secret with the imperial military liaison before dawn," Bennett explained, his gaze shifting between Chaitanya and Tejaswini. "The logistics of the academy cannot support five hundred new benders. More than that—the empire doesn't want students. It wants soldiers. They are raising the advancement thresholds to impossible levels. Anyone who fails the practical phases today is being stripped of academic standing."

"Stripped?" Tejaswini echoed, her eyes narrowing. "They'd expel people who just manifested yesterday?"

"Not just expelled," Bennett said grimly. "Reassigned. Non-bending disciples who showed no awakening during the Solstice are being given transport papers to the southern border fortifications to work as logistical laborers. Non-bending faculty—scholars who have taught classical philosophy and metallurgy here for thirty years—are receiving termination orders this morning. The academy is discarding anyone who cannot cast a blow."

"That's madness!" Anil exploded, his voice rising in fury as he slammed his hand down onto the cedar table. "My uncle sent his life savings so I could study engineering! Half the fourth-year class are scholars, not fighters! They can't just throw three hundred students into the mud because a rock entered the sky!"

"They can," Chaitanya said softly, his voice cutting through Anil’s rage with the cold clarity of slate. "And they will."

The room fell completely silent.

Chaitanya looked down at the red seal. "The empire has maintained peace for one hundred and ninety years because bending was scarce. When only a handful of families possessed power, order was easy to enforce through law and lineage. But if power is everywhere... if a farmer's son in the outer valley can suddenly call down lightning or tear up a road... the empire's monopoly is broken. The only way the crown survives is by seizing every capable weapon before someone else organizes them."

Bennett gave a slow, grim nod. "That is precisely what the military proctors are doing. The examination this afternoon has four elimination phases. If you fail a single phase, you are out."

"Then we have to prepare," Tejaswini said, her posture shifting in a fraction of a second from sleepy comrade to cold, battle-tested vanguard. "We can't walk into those rings blind. Bennett, what are the testing halls like?"

"Packed," Bennett said with a dry, bitter snort. "I came through the central colonnade ten minutes ago. Lines of terrified novices are stretching all the way to the refectory gates. People are fighting over the open rings. You can't even find a patch of gravel to stretch your legs."

"I'm going to the Research Wing first," Chaitanya said, standing up. "I need to see what Anirudh's records say about the evaluation criteria."

"Don't bother," Bennett warned. "The entire archive is under guard."

Chaitanya didn't answer. He threw his long slate tunic over his shoulders, slipped into his winter boots, and headed for the door. "Ten minutes. Meet me at the base of the Bell Tower."

***

The Archival Wing was worse than Bennett had described.

When Chaitanya reached the covered stone arcade, two imperial guardsmen in fluted iron breastplates were stationed at the bronze doors, their heavy polearms crossed to bar entry. 

Inside the vestibule, the scene was one of quiet devastation. 

Old Master Hemant, who had spent forty years cataloging the mineral densities of the Deadwinter rift, was standing on the gravel path holding a small wooden chest tied with frayed string. Two junior proctors were methodically searching his belongings, tossing bundles of dried medicinal herbs onto the frozen turf. The old scholar didn't argue. He simply stood with his head bowed, his thin grey beard trembling in the wind, a slip of red dismissal parchment clutched in his withered fingers.

Across the quadrangle, crowds of third-year students were shouting outside Training Hall One, shoving against one another to secure a practice slot. A boy in a scorched green smock was sitting against a stone pillar, clutching a broken wrist, weeping silently while his friends argued over who was to blame for the errant stone shard.

It was not an academy anymore. It was an ant hill that had been stepped on by an iron boot.

"Chaitanya!" 

A hand caught his sleeve from behind the colonnade. 

He turned to find Tejaswini, Bennett, and Anil huddled in the shadow of the great cedar buttresses. Tejaswini’s hood was pulled low over her brow, her breath pluming white against the dark wool.

"Did you get in?" Bennett asked quietly.

"Guarded by imperial regulars," Chaitanya said, shaking his head. "They're locking down the transit charts. The administration is cutting away the scholars to make room for barracks."

"And the training pits are impossible," Anil muttered, glancing back at the shouting mob in the courtyard. "If we stay out here, we won't throw a single strike before the proctors call our numbers. We'll fail by default."

"Not if we leave the grounds," Tejaswini said.

Bennett looked at her sharply. "Leave? The boundary gates are closed until sundown. Anyone caught climbing the perimeter wall is branded an imperial deserter."

"We don't need to climb the wall," she replied, her eyes flashing with dangerous confidence. "The southern park reserve. The old-growth forest where the first chancellors planted the white cedars."

"That area has been restricted for fifty years," Bennett hissed. "The undergrowth is wild, and the ravines run straight into the Deadwinter fault."

"Exactly," Tejaswini said, turning on her heel. "Which means no proctors, no crowds, and no guards. There’s an old drainage culvert behind the botanical greenhouse that leads directly past the palisade. A year ago, the seniors built a stone fire pit in a hidden clearing near the stream. The space is thirty paces wide, shielded by high limestone ledges."

She looked at Chaitanya, her gaze steady, challenging, and resolute. "Do we want to stand here waiting for the proctors to measure our coffins, or do we want to train?"

Chaitanya looked back at the old scholar sitting on his wooden chest in the dirt. He felt the cold band of iron on his left wrist, and beneath it, the quiet, humming elasticity of the air waiting for his command.

"Lead the way," Chaitanya said.

Without another word, the four of them pulled their cloaks tight, turned their backs on the hysterical courtyards of Tattva Academy, and melted into the shadow of the winter pines.
```
