# Chapter 18: The Shadow of the Bestiary

The sunken amphitheater of the Ordnance Hall smelled of pulverized slate, damp lime wash, and old tallow. Tiered semicircles of rough-hewn granite rose around a central demonstration floor where Master Gopinath paced behind a massive timber lectern. His right arm ended at the elbow in a capped leather stump studded with brass rivets; in his left hand he held an iron-tipped pointer five feet long.

Behind him hung four linen drop-cloths stretched across timber frames, covered in detailed charcoal schematics of dissected muscle, marrow, and bone.

"If you imagine the Breach yields animals," Gopinath began, his gravelly voice echoing off the curved stone ceiling, "you will die within five minutes of your first patrol. An animal hunts when hungry and flees when injured. The things that crawl out of the southern sinkholes do not hunger for meat. They hunt for animus. They kill to feed the core at the base of their spine."

He slammed the iron pointer against the first linen schematic. The cloth shuddered, releasing a puff of charcoal dust.

"Tier One: Rakshasa kin. Quadrupedal scavengers, packs of four to eight. Fast, agile, bone-crested craniums capable of deflecting common iron crossbow bolts. Their claws secrete a mild paralyzing grease that numbs human nerve endings upon contact. Do not attempt to grapple. A concentrated flame ribbon to the underbelly breaks their stride; an iron spear through the eye socket finishes the carcass."

The iron tip moved to the second canvas. The drawing depicted an elongated, gaunt humanoid torso with inverted knee joints and a ribcage splayed open like the teeth of an iron trap.

"Tier Two: The corpse-types. Pishachas and Vetalas. These are not beasts. They are necrotic condensations formed when the miasma settles into mass graves or unburned battlefields. They mimic human voices: cries of weeping children, calls for help from lost sentries. Their blood is black, thick as pitch, and caustic to steel. If a Vetala’s spinal marrow touches open skin, gangrene sets in within thirty breaths."

Gopinath paused, his rheumy eye sweeping across the tiered rows of cadets. "Normal wind dispersal will not kill them. You cannot blow away something that has no breath of its own. You must crush the cervical vertebrae or burn the corpse to white ash."

The pointer rose to the third canvas: a colossal, hulking shape resembling a bipedal rhinoceros with plates of layered chitin four inches thick.

"Tier Three: Danavas. Siege-beasts. Ten to fourteen cubits in height. Their mass alone can shatter a granite redoubt wall. They burrow through scree like river carp and generate seismic tremors with their forelegs. Only heavy ballista fire, deep earth-faults, or sustained, concentrated thermal lances can breach their outer shell."

He turned to the fourth canvas. It was blank, save for a single crimson ideogram painted in the center: *Asura*.

"Tier Four," Gopinath said, his voice dropping into a register that chilled the damp room. "You will pray you never see one. They possess intellect. They speak the primordial tongue of the Old Empire. They do not merely resist the elements; they bend them backward upon the caster. An Asura can turn a flame-bender’s heat into freezing necrosis inside their own arteries. If an Asura breaches the perimeter, the standing order for Candidate Units is not engagement. It is delayed withdrawal and immediate detonation of the signal beacons."

The latch at the rear of the amphitheater clicked open.

Chaitanya turned his head along the stone bench.

Tejaswini stepped down the granite stairs. She had changed out of her travel clothes into full combat rig: lacquered black leather arm-guards over crimson wool, her cavalry sabre strapped across her back, iron greaves buckled over her boots. Her hair was bound in a fresh braid, tight against her scalp. There was no fatigue on her brow, only the sharp, unblinking focus of an archer drawing to the cheek.

She slid onto the bench beside Chaitanya without a word. Her armor carried the scent of mountain road and cold iron.

"The council?" Chaitanya whispered under the cover of Gopinath’s rasping lecture.

"The levies are signed," she murmured back, her eyes fixed on the blank fourth canvas. "My father gave me his personal whetstone and three bottles of anti-venom tallow. We deploy with the vanguard, as planned."

Before Chaitanya could reply, the iron warning bell atop the Ordnance Hall sounded.

It was not the rhythmic, four-stroke cadence of muster. It was a single, long, continuous tolling that rattled the lead-framed glass of the high clerestory windows.

Master Gopinath lowered his iron pointer. A harsh, humorless grin split his weathered face, revealing yellowed teeth.

"That is your call, cubs," the old veteran said, tapping his leather stump against the wood. "Clear your desks. Leave your notebooks. Report to the Lower Quarry in full kit within ten minutes. Your proctors are waiting."

***

The Lower Quarry was a vast, sunken scar carved into the grey limestone cliffs two miles south of the Academy. Fifty paces across, the pit was littered with towering blocks of quarried stone, heaps of loose scree, and iron-spiked barricades designed to simulate the shattered redoubts of the southern frontier.

The wind howled down through the rock cuts, whipping slate dust into spiraling devils across the arena floor.

Unit 07 stood at the northern entrance gate, iron-rimmed shields unslung, weapons drawn. Bennett held his heavy partisan in a two-handed grip, the greased blade dull grey in the overcast light. Anil held two brass-tipped throwing darts in his left hand, bouncing lightly on the balls of his feet to shake the nervous tension from his calves. Tejaswini’s hand rested on the wire-wound grip of her sabre, her aura humming beneath the surface like an enclosed kiln.

Chaitanya stood at the center of the formation, his hands empty, his balance settled low.

Twenty paces ahead, at the center of the blood-stone circle, two figures waited.

Proctor Rao stood like an iron statue. The veteran earth-master wore plates of jointed basalt strapped over boiled ox-hide, his massive shoulders broad as a draft horse. In his right hand, he rested a five-foot square-headed war maul on the rock, its stone head already covered in chips and dried stains from a decade of border skirmishes.

Beside him stood Proctor Meera. She was tall, lean, and lithe as a hunting hound, clad in form-fitting grey leather reinforced at the joints with flexible steel mesh. At each hip rested a twenty-inch baton bound in coiled copper wire. The air around her shoulders vibrated with an unnatural shimmer; faint blue sparks clicked and snapped against the copper rivets of her collar, smelling of rain-scorched ozone.

"Unit Seven," Rao’s voice boomed across the quarry floor, deep enough to vibrate the loose pebbles at Chaitanya’s boots. "You were given candidate ribbons. You were given iron rations. Today, we determine whether you are fit to hold a trench or if you are meat for the Pishachas."

Meera took half a step forward. Her eyes were pale grey, like storm clouds over the northern ocean. She drew both copper batons with a single, fluid snap. The air between the metal rods ionized immediately, a bright blue arc of lightning dancing between the tips with a crack like a whip.

"No practice paddings," Meera said, her voice thin, razor-sharp, and devoid of warmth. "No holding back. You hold this circle against us for twelve minutes by the hourglass, or you return to the capital on foot as supply draymen."

Bennett stepped forward, bringing his spearhead level with Rao’s chest. "We are ready, Proctor."

Rao lifted his stone maul from the dirt with one arm, resting the heavy head against his shoulder.

"Then draw your steel, cubs," Rao growled. "Because the monsters at the Breach will not ask your names before they break your ribs."
