# Chapter 4: Attire for the Darkest Night

On the evening of the Winter Solstice, the academy did not celebrate. It prepared to listen.

Across the four wings, the routine noise of drills and lecture chimes faded earlier than usual. The iron gates to the sparring pits were chained; the forge fires were banked with wet coal dust, sending up thin, pale ribbons of cedar smoke that mingled with the gathering mist. 

Inside their shared quarters, the light was shrinking.

Chaitanya stood near the wooden pillar between his sleeping mat and the hearth, adjusting the drape of his coat. 

It was a garment he rarely took from the bottom of his cedar chest: a long coat of dense, matte-black wool that fell well below the knees, cut with straight, severe vertical seams that carried no ornamentation. The fabric was heavy enough to hold its discipline against mountain gales, falling in clean, layered folds from his shoulders to his ankles. Beneath it, a high-collared tunic of deep slate linen fit close to his neck, smooth and unembroidered, secured at the waist by a narrow sash of blackened hemp. Around his left wrist rested the plain band of dull iron, cold and unyielding against his skin.

He had no insignia on his chest. No house crest, no collegiate bars, no marks of faction. In the dim lantern light, the silhouette was reserved, dignified, and austere, suited less for a young bender and more for a silent counselor walking the quiet margins of history.

Anil, leaning against the timber doorframe while tying the laces of his winter boots, gave a low, appreciative whistle. 

“You clean up well,” Anil said, shaking his head with a grin. “Didn't know you had anything in that chest besides moldy resonance treatises.”

Chaitanya tugged the small horn toggle at his throat into place. “Neither did I.”

Two paces away, Bennett stood before the polished bronze plate hanging beside the wash basin. His presence was lighter, calmer, yet equally disciplined. Bennett wore a coat of medium-weight, pale blue wool that fell just above his knees, structured with clean, tailored lines that allowed his arms full freedom of motion. Beneath it, an inner tunic in a darker sea-blue rose to a high, structured neckline, cinched at the waist by a narrow sash of pale silver-blue hemp. At his wrist was a band of carved river-horn, and his boots were polished calfskin, greased against the snow.

Bennett gave his silver sash two firm, precise tugs, inspecting his reflection with quiet satisfaction.

“The wind off the eastern ridge will be biting by the time the lamps go down,” Bennett said, turning toward Anil. “Button the inner flap, Anil. If you spend the unlit hour shivering, you’ll disturb the whole circle.”

Anil rolled his eyes, but obediently fastened the horn toggles of his own coat: a comfortable, mid-length piece of deep charcoal wool lined with unbleached sheep’s fleece, warm and unpretentious. “I don't shiver, Bennett. Airbenders regulate their micro-climate. It’s an advanced somatic technique.”

“You shivered through the entire third frost last month,” Bennett remarked mildly.

“That was a physiological recalibration,” Anil shot back, grinning as he grabbed his heavy wool scarf.

The door to the inner alcove opened, and Tejaswini stepped into the room.

The banter died instantly.

She had set aside the red light armor and the black fingerless gloves. Tonight, she wore a high-collared winter mantle of deep madder-red wool, layered over a tunic of dark charcoal linen that fell in soft, heavy folds to the tops of her polished leather boots. The sleeves were long and lined with dark fur at the wrists, protecting her hands without the need for gloves. Her thick, dark hair was bound up in a severe, elegant braid pinned with two carved copper needles, leaving the graceful line of her neck exposed to the cool draft of the room.

She did not look like an academy brawler who had nearly scorched an oak tree to ash that morning. She looked regal, solemn, and breathtakingly complete.

Her hazel eyes swept the room. They passed over Anil’s wide grin, over Bennett’s respectful nod, and came to rest on Chaitanya.

Chaitanya did not look away. But his throat tightened.

The cold iron band around his wrist suddenly burned cold against his pulse. He saw the faint, nervous tension in her throat, the way her hand rose unconsciously to check the copper needles in her hair, checking to see if he had noticed. She was looking at him with that same quiet question she had carried all morning: a hope so fragile and immense that speaking of it was holding flame near dry straw.

He swallowed the ache, bowed his head a fraction of an inch, and kept his face smooth as still water.

“If Bennett checks that sash again,” Tejaswini said, breaking the silence before the tension could snap, her voice dry and teasing though her cheeks held a faint, telltale flush, “the unlit hour will pass before we reach the ridge.”

Bennett gave a small, dignified nod. “The sash is set. The water skins are packed.”

“Then we go,” Chaitanya said softly.

He reached for the timber latch, pulling the heavy door outward.

***

The corridors of the dormitory wing were bathed in the Dimming Hour.

Every seven paces, junior proctors using long brass snuffers were lowering the wicks of the tallow lanterns. The bright, yellow glare of daytime was fading into a warm, hushed amber glow that pooled along the stone floorboards. Conversations in the hallways were low, almost whispered. Students moved in small, self-contained groups of three and four, their footsteps muffled by heavy wool socks and felted winter boots.

There was no shouting. No boasting about exam marks. 

The Solstice Vigil was the oldest tradition in the provinces, rooted in a time before the academies, when the first survivors of the Great Comet had gathered in the pitch-black freeze of Deadwinter simply to remind one another that humanity had not yet gone cold. 

As the four of them stepped through the northern portal of the dormitory archway, the mountain air struck their faces.

It was crisp, clean, and carrying the bitter, ozone scent of high-altitude frost. Above them, the winter sky was an immense dome of dark indigo, clear and cold, dusted with thousands of sharp, uncaring stars. The crescent moon hung pale and fragile near the western crags, throwing long, faint shadows across the snow-dusted paths.

Down in the valley, hundreds of figures were already moving along the winding mountain trail that climbed toward the Great Ridge: lanterns bobbing like fireflies in the deep dark, silent and synchronized.

Anil adjusted his wool scarf, breathing out a cloud of white vapor that caught the starlight. “Every year I forget how big the sky looks when the lanterns go out.”

Bennett fell into step beside him, his boots crunching rhythmically against the frozen grit of the path. “That’s the point of the vigil, Anil. To remind you that you’re small.”

Tejaswini walked a half-step behind them, her hands tucked deep into the fur-lined cuffs of her red mantle. 

Chaitanya fell into place beside her. 

He didn't speak. He didn't offer a hand. But as their boots struck the frozen earth in unison, her shoulder brushed lightly against his arm: once, twice, steady and deliberate: and she did not pull away.

Ahead of them, the path rose steeply into the silent dark of the mountains.
