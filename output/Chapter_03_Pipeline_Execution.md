## 📖 STAGE 4: THE RENDERED DEFINITIVE CHAPTER PROSE

```markdown
By the time Chaitanya reached the flagstones of the residential quadrangle, the sun had dropped behind the western watchtowers, leaving the sky the color of bruised slate. The wind off the river had sharpened, carrying the faint, metallic bite of night-frost.

Ahead of him, two silhouettes were moving down the lantern-lit arcade toward the dormitory gate. 

One walked with long, deliberate strides, his shoulders level and his hands tucked into the pockets of a light-blue coat. The other was practically bouncing on the balls of his boots, his wool scarf flapping over one shoulder like a frayed banner, turning sideways every few paces to make an animated point with his hands.

“Anil,” Chaitanya called out.

Anil spun on his heel, caught his footing on a frosted flagstone with a momentary wobble, and grinned broadly. “Speak of the scholar. Where were you? We thought Nikos buried you under a collapsed codex.”

Bennett turned more slowly, his pace evening out as Chaitanya closed the distance. Even in the gloom, Bennett looked put together—his dark blue inner collar straight, his structured wool coat buttoned to the chest, though the leather wraps around his forearms were still damp from the river.

“Nikos was sorting agricultural tax rolls from before the flood,” Chaitanya said, matching their stride. “He wasn't in a hurry.”

“You missed the ice,” Anil said, falling in on Chaitanya’s left. “Clean as polished glass from the weir all the way down to the millrace. Not a soul out there. We had three miles of frozen water to ourselves.”

Chaitanya glanced at Bennett. “Three miles?”

Bennett let out a quiet huff through his nose, his breath misting grey in the cold air. “Two miles. And Anil spent the first hour trying to slide backward on his boots instead of holding the perimeter current.”

“It’s called kinetic adaptation,” Anil protested, nudging Chaitanya’s shoulder. “You have to know how the friction gives way beneath you. It’s practical research.”

“It’s called almost fracturing your tailbone on the mill stones,” Bennett said without missing a beat. “Twice.”

Anil made an exaggerated scowl, flicked his wrist in a sharp, casual motion as though trying to gather an air pocket to trip Bennett’s heel. Nothing happened; the air remained undisturbed in the evening chill. Anil looked down at his bare knuckles, laughed at himself, and shoved his hands back into his pockets. “Fine. Point taken. Bennett made me drill three-point stance turns until my calves locked up.”

The three of them laughed, the sound rising easily into the quiet quadrangle. 

It was familiar. It was safe. Walking between them, with Anil’s restless energy on one flank and Bennett’s unmoving gravity on the other, the dark timber vaults of the archives and the terrifying handwriting on the stolen plate felt distant.

“Did you see Tejaswini before she went to the kiln master?” Anil asked, his tone shifting from mockery to genuine, unfiltered awe. “Fire bending. Actually breathing sparks off her knuckles. Four years of grinding through those miserable texts, and she just cracked it wide open.”

“I saw,” Chaitanya murmured.

“Clean, too,” Bennett added, his deep voice carrying that quiet, measured respect he reserved for true discipline. “No blowback on her forearms. No thermal runaway in her pulse. When she ignited, her balance stayed rooted in her hips. Most novices blow themselves backward on their first ignition. She looked like she’d been drilling the form for months.”

“She’s going to be impossible to live with now,” Anil chuckled, shaking his head. “You know how she gets when she has an edge over us. She’s going to demand the big stool at the table every night.”

“She earned the stool,” Bennett said simply.

“She did,” Chaitanya agreed.

He said it quietly, but he meant it. Standing between Bennett and Anil, hearing the honest pride in their voices, the bitter knot of inadequacy that usually tightened in his throat loosened just a fraction. They were not competing for thrones. They were just four orphans and commoners thrown into the same cold stone barracks, trying to survive the curriculum.

They reached the heavy oak door of their suite. 

Bennett pushed it open, the iron hinges groaning softly against the threshold. 

The heat of the room hit them first. In the corner hearth, river stones that Bennett had hauled from the bank two days ago lay stacked around a bed of glowing charcoal, radiating a steady, deep warmth that kept the damp from creeping up between the floorboards. 

On the low cedar table in the center of the common space, an iron kettle was already simmering over a small brass burner, the smell of boiled turnips, cracked barley, and dried mountain salt filling the air. There were four wooden bowls laid out on the scrubbed pine surface, alongside four pairs of horn spoons.

Sitting on a low timber stool near the hearth was Tejaswini.

She had changed out of her structured red training armor into a loose, comfortable tunic of unbleached linen, though her dark trousers remained tucked into her boots. Her black fingerless gloves lay neatly folded on the side table by her ink stone. Her long hair was tied back in a careless knot, and she was watching the kettle with that sharp, attentive focus she brought to everything.

She looked up as the door closed.

Her gaze swept over Anil, over Bennett, and landed squarely on Chaitanya.

The room held its breath. 

“You’re late,” she said. But the edge had gone out of her voice. Her hazel eyes were soft, questioning, checking him for whatever had driven him out into the cold two hours ago.

“Varma’s lecture ran long,” Chaitanya said smoothly, unfastening the dark sash at his waist and hanging his heavy charcoal coat on the wall peg near his sleeping mat.

Underneath, against his ribs, his hand slipped into his inner pocket. With practiced, silent precision, he slid the cool brass aperture casing free and tucked it into the small, locked wooden cedar box resting beneath his rush pallet, sliding the box under the pine slats with the heel of his boot.

*Safe.*

He walked over to the hearth, picked up the earthenware pitcher from the stone ledge, and poured three cups of cold well-water, handing one to Bennett and one to Anil before taking a seat on the low bench across from Tejaswini.

“How did Master Pyraxis take the report?” Chaitanya asked, holding her gaze steadily this time, allowing himself to look at her without the shield of scholarly detachment.

Tejaswini took a wooden cup, her fingers brushing against his knuckles for a heartbeat before she pulled back to cradle the clay.

“He didn't say good,” she replied, a faint, wry smile curving her lips. “He said *adequate*. Then he told me to double my dawn drills so I don't embarrass his registry.”

Anil groaned loudly, dropping onto a floor cushion with his back against the wall. “That man is a monster. If I ever awaken lightning, I’m going to singe his mustache.”

“If you awaken lightning,” Bennett said, ladling the thick, fragrant barley broth into the four bowls, “we’re moving your sleeping mat to the balcony.”

The room erupted into laughter again. 

Chaitanya took his bowl of steaming broth, blowing across the surface as the heat warmed his face. Outside the unglazed window slits, the first night bell began to toll across Tattva Academy—slow, heavy, resonant strokes that marked the passing of the day.

Tomorrow, the Solstice preparations would begin. Tomorrow, the celestial moon would shift in the sky.

But tonight, in the lantern light, the four of them sat shoulder to shoulder on the low timber floor, sharing salt and broth in the only home they had ever known.
```
