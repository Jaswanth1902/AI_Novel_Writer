# Chapter 31: The Morning of Unspoken Questions

The next morning did not begin like the others.

The fourth bell of the watchtower rang its usual four strokes, the brass notes falling through the morning mist and dying against the cedar roofs of the dormitory quadrangle. In the third-tier barracks room, neither Bennett nor Anil moved for a long time after the final strike faded.

The room smelled of stale vinegar liniment, wood smoke from the unlit iron stove, and damp wool drying on cedar pegs.

Bennett sat up on his pallet with agonizing slowness. Every breath was a short, guarded calculation: three broken ribs pressed against the linen bindings wrapped tight around his chest. He swung his legs over the edge of the straw mattress, resting his forearms on his knees, his jaw locked tight until the dull, sickening throb in his side subsided into a steady ache.

Across the room, Anil was already awake.

The younger cadet sat on his footlocker, pulling on his left boot with careful, deliberate tugs. He did not toss his throwing darts at the pine door. He did not whistle or complain about the kitchen rations. When his heel slid home into the stiff leather, he simply sat with his head down, staring at the floorboards between his boots.

"How are the shins?" Bennett asked, his voice thick with sleep and gravel.

"Tolerable," Anil answered quietly. "The swelling went down after the ice compresses. I can run if I have to."

"You don't have to run today." Bennett pushed himself to his feet with the aid of his pine crutch, his boots finding the floor. "Today, we walk."

They buckled their grey candidate tunics in silence. For the first time since entering the Academy, neither of them checked the inspection polish on their iron buckles. The metal was dull, scratched by flying limestone scree, the wool still carrying the faint, sweet smell of burnt beast tallow that no washing would ever fully remove.

When they stepped out into the central corridor, the change in the Academy hit them like a wall of cold iron.

Cadets from the junior classes had filled the hallway on their way to the morning mess hall. At the sight of Bennett leaning on his crutch and Anil with his bandaged shins, the murmur of two dozen voices vanished.

Cadets pressed themselves flat against the stone walls to clear the center of the hall. Eyes traveled down to Bennett’s splintered gear, then to the dark crimson stains on the cuff of Anil’s sleeve. No one spoke. No one offered a casual greeting. The younger boys lowered their heads with a frightened, reverent deference usually reserved for senior preceptors returning from the frontier trenches.

Bennett kept his gaze straight ahead, his crutch striking the stone flagstones with a measured, rhythmic thud: *clack, clack, clack.*

They crossed the open quadrangle to the medical wing.

The inner sanctuary was a vaulted limestone chamber built into the foundation of the central tower, isolated from the common hospital wards. At the heavy oak door stood two sentries of the Veteran Guard: men in their thirties wearing fluted iron breastplates and carrying seven-foot halberds. The black-iron tokens of Proctor Rao were pinned to their leather gorgets.

At Bennett’s approach, the sentries did not raise their weapons. The left-hand guard simply reached out with a gauntleted thumb, lifted the heavy iron latch, and pulled the door open six inches.

"Ten minutes, squad leader," the sentry muttered through his visor. "Rao’s orders."

Bennett and Anil stepped inside.

The room was cold, lit only by a slit window high in the southern wall that let in a single diagonal shaft of grey morning light. The air smelled of dried yarrow, camphor, and the bitter tang of crushed willow bark.

Chaitanya lay on a narrow bed of split pine, covered to his collar in grey woolen blankets.

His breathing was deeper now than it had been on the railway platform, his chest rising and falling forty times an hour with the slow, unbroken rhythm of a mountain stream. The splints on his left shoulder held his arm completely immobile; the skin of his face, while still pale as parchment, had lost the grey, corpse-like hue of the previous night.

Tejaswini sat on a three-legged wooden stool beside the bed.

She had not changed out of her uniform. Her crimson cloak lay folded over the back of the stool; her sleeves were rolled up to her elbows, revealing forearms bruised purple where the Danava’s shockwave had hammered her guard. Her dark hair was loose, falling in tangled strands over her shoulders, her cheekbones sharp and hollowed by thirty hours without sleep.

Her hands rested flat on the blanket near Chaitanya’s hip, her fingertips touching the rough wool.

"Has he stirred?" Bennett asked, leaning on his crutch at the foot of the bed.

Tejaswini shook her head once, a single, sharp motion. "His eyes moved beneath the lids at fourth watch. His pulse is sixty cycles. The internal heat is holding without my aid."

Anil stepped up to the edge of the mattress, his gaze lingering on the dark purple bruising that ran down Chaitanya’s neck into the shoulder bindings. "The preceptors came by while you were down here?"

"Twice," Tejaswini said, her voice dropping into a register of cold, dangerous silk. "A magistrate from the Ministry of Inscription wanted to examine the residue on his boots. Rao’s guards met them at the threshold with leveled steel. They did not enter."

She looked up at Bennett, her amber eyes burning with an intense, unyielding fire that sleep deprivation could not diminish.

"They know something happened in that street, Bennett," she said softly. "A Tier Three Danava does not die of an arrow wound. The entire Academy is holding its breath."

Bennett looked down at Chaitanya’s motionless face, his hand tightening around the pine head of his crutch.

"Let them hold it," Bennett said. "When he wakes up, we will be ready."
