# Chapter 29: The Pyres of Morning

Morning arrived over Langford without warmth.

A pale, leaden sheet of grey light crept over the mountains, exposing what the night had torn apart. Collapsed walls of timber and river stone lined the main thoroughfare like broken teeth. Cobblestones were churned into a red, freezing muck mixed with congealed black marrow, shattered bone crests, and broken spear shafts.

In the center of the avenue lay the Danava.

The twelve-ton carcass looked like a fallen obsidian monolith, half-veiled in wisps of freezing vapor and black smoke that still rose from its shattered thorax. Around it, crews of surviving miners in soot-blackened leather aprons dragged dead Rakshasas into three massive pyres. When the torches touched the tallow-soaked wood, the pyres roared into greasy orange flame, sending black, foul-smelling smoke spiraling into the mountain mist.

Inside the ruined grain storehouse, the air was cold, smelling of vinegar bandages, damp flour paste, and dried blood.

Bennett sat on an upturned nail keg near the doorway. His torso was bound tight in strips of boiled linen soaked in wine vinegar, bracing the three broken ribs that creaked with every shallow breath he drew. His left arm rested in a sling fashioned from an oilcloth mantle. Between his knees he held a rough pine crutch, his square, bruised face fixed on the door in silent, unyielding vigilance.

Beside him, Anil leaned against a timber pillar, a wooden bowl of hot water in his hands. Both his shins were wrapped in dark woolen strips where the storehouse rafters had crushed the flesh, but he stood on his own feet, his agile frame taut with nervous exhaustion.

At the rear of the storehouse, on a pallet of clean straw covered by two heavy wool blankets, lay Chaitanya.

His face was pale as river chalk. A dark crust of dried blood lined the rims of his nostrils and the lobes of his ears. His left shoulder sat an inch lower than the right, immobilized by wooden splints bound with hemp cord. His chest barely moved: thirty shallow, quiet cycles an hour, his pulse a faint, fluttering thread beneath his wrist.

Tejaswini knelt beside the straw.

She had refused food. She had refused the clean water brought by the town matron. Her crimson travel woolens were torn at the elbow, coated in soot, lime dust, and the dark splatter of beast bile. Her hands, red and blistered from the heat of her incandescent sabre, held Chaitanya’s right hand pressed between her palms.

Through her skin, she maintained a steady, unbroken current of core warmth.

It was not fire. It was the lowest, most delicate vibration of the southern flame discipline: thermal conduction calibrated to match the blood temperature of a living human. The steady heat seeped into Chaitanya’s freezing fingers, traveling up his forearms to keep his heart from slowing into irreversible hypothermic arrest.

Her eyes were sunken, shadowed by purple bruises of fatigue, yet they never left his face.

When the local militia doctor stepped into the alcove with an iron probe and a jar of black leech-salve, Tejaswini’s head snapped up. Her amber eyes flared with such cold, lethal aristocratic ferocity that the man froze mid-step, bowed his head in terror, and retreated out into the street without uttering a syllable.

"His pulse is holding," Bennett said from the doorway, his voice low and gravelly. "The breathing is deeper than it was at third watch."

Tejaswini did not look up. Her thumbs gently stroked the cold knuckles in her grip. "His internal conduits are burned. He pulled energy from somewhere that has no name in the manuals. If his temperature drops below thirty degrees, the marrow will freeze in his bones."

The gravel outside crunched under heavy footsteps.

Lyra stepped through the shattered doorway. Her left arm was bound in splints of split cedar, her wide-brimmed hat missing, her dark leather coat torn across the ribs. In her right hand she carried an iron dispatch tube sealed with the triple-ring stamp of the Frontier Vanguard Command.

She stopped at the edge of the straw pallet.

The cynical sneer that had defined her face since their arrival on the railway platform was gone, replaced by a quiet, somber reverence that belonged to soldiers standing over their dead.

"The train is ready," Lyra said. "The engine has a full head of steam. Five hundred miners, eighty women, and sixty orphans are aboard the flatcars. The southern tracks are clear all the way to the Academy junction."

Bennett leaned on his pine crutch, straightening his spine against the wall. "And the beast horde?"

"Scattered," Lyra answered. "The moment the Danava’s core imploded, the corpse-types lost their link. The Rakshasas tore each other apart in the creek beds before retreating into the southern sinkholes. The Oakhaven Basin is clear for the first time in ten years."

She knelt on one knee beside the pallet and laid the iron dispatch tube on Chaitanya’s chest.

"The official action report for Commandant Varis," Lyra said, looking at Tejaswini. "Unit Zero-Seven is credited with the unassisted defense of Langford and the first recorded field kill of a Tier Three siege-beast by a candidate detachment in forty years."

Tejaswini looked at the dispatch, then up into the scout's weathered face. "And the nature of his strike?"

Lyra held Tejaswini’s gaze for five long seconds. Her eyes moved to the shattered breastplate of the Danava visible through the broken doorway, where the iron-like chitin had exploded outward from internal vacuum collapse.

"The report states that the candidate vanguard utilized a coordinated high-explosive sulfur charge and synchronized thermal focus to breach the thoracic cavity," Lyra said, her voice flat, official, and ironclad. "The Ministry of Inscription asks questions when things do not fit their fourfold books. I have no love for the Ministry."

Tejaswini inclined her head by a fraction of an inch: a silent, profound acknowledgment between warriors.

"Thank you, Operative Lyra."

Lyra stood up, turning to Anil and Bennett. "Bring the stretcher. The coal cars leave before the valley wind turns."

Anil and Bennett brought forward a litter made of two spruce poles and heavy canvas. Working together with agonizing slowness, they lifted Chaitanya from the straw, transferring his broken frame to the canvas without disturbing his splinted shoulder.

Tejaswini walked beside the litter, her fingers never leaving Chaitanya’s hand, her warmth flowing into his skin as they carried him out through the smoking pyres toward the waiting iron rails.
