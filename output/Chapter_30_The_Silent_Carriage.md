# Chapter 30: The Silent Carriage

The iron wheels kept time against the rail joints: *clack-clack, clack-clack.*

The coal transport climbed north through the snowy defiles of the Oakhaven Gap, its heavy boiler rods working with a rhythmic, mechanical groan that vibrated through the oak floorboards of the passenger coach. Outside the slit-windows, the jagged basalt walls were plastered with fresh snow, the pine trees bent double beneath the weight of winter rime.

Inside the carriage, nobody spoke.

Chaitanya lay on the split-pine bench along the eastern wall. His head rested on a bundled sheepskin mantle, his body wrapped in three coarse wool blankets stained at the collar with vinegar liniment. His face had settled into the stillness of marble: pale, unmoving, his pulse beating in slow, measured intervals like water dripping into an empty cistern.

Tejaswini sat on the floorboards beside him, her back braced against the iron framing of the wall.

Her hands had not left his. Her blistered palms remained wrapped around his cold knuckles, maintaining that faint, unbroken thread of core warmth that kept the chill of the mountain air from reaching his heart. Her eyelids were heavy, lined with dark circles of sheer physical exhaustion, yet every time the locomotive lurched on a frozen curve, her fingers tightened instinctively around his wrist.

Across the aisle, Bennett sat with his boots planted wide, his pine crutch leaning against his knee. He held the broken socket of his partisan in his lap, turning the jagged ash timber over and over in his calloused fingers.

"A Tier Three siege-beast," Bennett said at last. His voice was a rasp that barely carried over the rumble of the engine. "My grandfather told stories of the Great Danava of the Red Valley. It took three companies of the Imperial Heavy Line, two siege ballistas, and sixteen barrels of burning pitch to crack its carapace. Half of them died before the beast fell."

He looked across the narrow space at the silent boy on the bench.

"And Chaitanya broke it with an open palm."

Anil sat by the cold cast-iron stove, his head bowed, his hands resting motionless on his wrapped shins. The flippancy that had carried him through the candidate halls was gone, burned away by the stench of burning beast pyres and the scream of splintering storehouse walls.

"He didn't just break it," Anil whispered, looking up with bloodshot eyes. "He stood between it and Tejaswini. He knew what twelve tons would do to human ribs, and he stepped into the gap anyway."

Tejaswini did not look away from Chaitanya’s profile. Her thumb stroked the curve of his knuckles with a quiet, fierce tenderness.

"You both know what that means," she said softly.

Her voice was not loud, but it carried the cold, absolute authority of House Varma’s ancient ancestral oaths. Bennett and Anil looked at her.

"The Inscription manuals teach that air cannot ignite without fire," Tejaswini said, her dark eyes rising to meet Bennett’s, then Anil’s. "They teach that no human can manipulate pressure without somatic seals. If the Grand Preceptors or the Ministry of Inscription discover that Chaitanya bypassed the Four Gates, they will not reward him with medals. They will brand him an abomination against the First Emperor. They will dissect his nervous system in the Imperial Foundry."

She leaned forward, the shadow of her frame falling across the bench.

"We are Unit Zero-Seven," she said, each syllable carved from iron. "Whatever we saw on the cobblestones of Langford dies with us. To the instructors, to the proctors, to the cadets: the Danava fell to a coordinated sulfur detonation and my own thermal lance. Swear it on your blood."

Bennett did not hesitate. He reached to his belt, drew his iron utility knife with his uninjured right hand, and pressed the edge against the pad of his thumb. A bead of dark blood welled on the skin. He reached across the aisle and pressed his thumb against the iron socket of his broken spear.

"On my blood and the honor of the Iron Range," Bennett said solemnly. "The secret holds."

Anil drew his throwing dart, nicked his own thumb, and pressed his blood beside Bennett’s. "On my life. The secret holds."

Tejaswini bowed her head in silent assent.

The train’s whistle screamed twice: a high, piercing wail that echoed through the northern valley. The brakes groaned, steel biting into iron as the train slowed to a walking pace.

They had reached the Academy junction.

When the heavy oak door of the coach slid back, the cold air of the candidate quadrangle poured in.

The platform was lined with three hundred people.

Cadets from the first and second tiers stood shoulder to shoulder along the gravel sidings, their faces pale and stunned in the overcast light. Behind them stood instructors in dark wool coats, senior evaluators, and a dozen medical attendants holding two spruce litters.

Word of the Langford dispatch had arrived three hours earlier via the military telegraph. The news that four candidates had held the southern mining basin against a Danava incursion had spread like wild-fire through every barrack and mess hall.

Proctor Rao stood at the front of the platform, his massive frame clad in jointed basalt armor, his arms folded across his chest. Beside him stood Master Gopinath, leaning heavily on his iron-tipped pointer.

Bennett was the first to step down onto the platform, his pine crutch biting into the snow, his linen-bound ribs held rigid against the pain. Anil followed, limping on his wrapped shins, but carrying the squad’s canvas packs on his shoulders.

Then came the litter.

Four medical orderlies carried Chaitanya through the carriage door. He lay motionless, wrapped in grey blankets, the blood dried on his temples, his broken shoulder bound in cedar splints.

Tejaswini walked beside the litter, her uniform torn and blackened with soot, her cavalry sabre sheathed at her hip. She did not look at the three hundred cadets who stared in breathless awe. She did not look at the instructors. Her hand never released Chaitanya’s fingers until the orderlies lifted the litter onto their shoulders.

Proctor Rao stepped forward, his heavy boots crunching in the snow.

He looked down at Chaitanya’s pale face, then at the iron dispatch tube clutched in Bennett’s hand, sealed with the three rings of the Frontier Command.

The scarred earth-master slowly raised his right hand to the center of his chest, forming the formal fist-and-palm salute of the Imperial Vanguard: an honor reserved only for veterans of the southern trenches.

"Unit Zero-Seven," Rao’s voice boomed across the silent platform, deep enough to vibrate the snow on the carriage roofs. "You held the line."

He turned to the medical attendants, his jaw set like an iron vise.

"Take him to the inner sanctuary of the infirmary," Rao commanded. "Post two sentries from the Veteran Guard at the door. No preceptors. No magistrates. Anyone who enters without my personal token will be thrown from the western ramparts."

The attendants lifted the litter, carrying Chaitanya through the parting sea of cadets toward the high stone arches of the Academy.

Tejaswini walked at his side, her head held high, the soot on her cheekbones worn like war-paint as they passed into the shadow of the fortress.
