# Chapter 17: The Four Vanguard

Four days passed with the mechanical regularity of an iron clockwork.

Every dawn began at the fourth ring of the watchtower bell. The candidate vanguard ran the perimeter road: three miles of rutted dirt and crushed river gravel that hugged the high defensive palisade of the Academy. Breath plumed white in the freezing morning mist, lungs burning with the sharp taste of pine smoke from the valley charcoal pits. After the run came the conditioning yard: lifting hundred-pound granite rollers onto wooden trestles, twenty sets of five, followed by balance drills along suspended oak beams greased with tallow.

Chaitanya fell into the rhythm with cold, quiet precision. His muscles absorbed the strain without complaint, his breathing locked to the steady, unvarying rhythm he had maintained since childhood.

By the afternoon of the fourth day, the sky over the western mountains had cleared to a pale, brittle azure. On the sheltered porch of the candidate armory, four rough pine benches sat arranged around a low sharpening bench.

Bennett sat hunched over a three-foot iron-headed partisan, a rectangular Arkansas oilstone held steady between two broad, calloused thumbs. With slow, even passes, he drew the stone along the bevel of the spearhead, the rhythmic *shhk-shhk* of lubricated iron cutting through the quiet yard. A faint smell of neatsfoot oil and mineral grease rose from his leather apron.

"A dull blade bounces off a Rakshasa’s shoulder plate," Bennett said without looking up from his work. "Then the beast takes your forearm off at the elbow while you are wondering why your strike failed. Keep the edge at thirty degrees. Anything thinner rolls on bone."

Across from him, Anil sat cross-legged on a bench, tossing a heavy brass-weighted throwing dart into the air and catching it by the tail-feathers. A strip of dried mutton stuck out from the corner of his mouth, tough as cured leather.

"If a Rakshasa gets within spear-reach of me," Anil muttered, chewing vigorously, "the battle is already lost. I am a scout, not a castle gate. My job is to be thirty feet up a spruce tree dropping sulfur darts into its ear-hole."

"And if the spruce tree is on fire?" Bennett asked, wiping the blade with an oily rag. "Or if the beast tears the trunk down with two swipes of its foreclaws? You carry an iron short-sword for a reason, Anil. Sharpen it."

Anil sighed, tucking his dart into a wrist bracer, and reached down into his canvas kit-bag for his whetstone.

At the end of the bench, Tejaswini sat with her back to the timber wall. She wore travel leathers instead of her cadet linens: dark deer-hide lined with boiled wool, dyed the deep crimson of House Varma. A bronze-buckled sword-belt held a straight-bladed cavalry sabre at her left hip, its pommel shaped into the stylized head of a roaring stag.

Her fingers rested lightly on the brass fittings of a small leather travel trunk placed beside her boots.

A heavy carriage rolled into the gravel courtyard thirty paces away. It was an imperial-pattern landau built of black-varnished oak, its iron-shod wheels rattling on the flagstones. Two heavy grey geldings pulled the harness, their flanks steaming in the chill air. A liveried driver wearing the crimson and gold sash of House Varma held the reins, his posture stiff as an iron rod.

Anil paused with his whetstone raised. "The nobility travels in style. Four horses, sheepskin seat cushions, and an escort with silver-chased lance heads."

"Two horses," Tejaswini corrected quietly, rising from the bench. She picked up her travel trunk with one hand, the leather strap groaning under the weight of her gear. "And the seat cushions are horsehair, not sheepskin. My father dislikes softness in any form."

Bennett stood, nodding with blunt northern deference. "Thirty-six hours, Lady Varma?"

"Thirty-six hours," she replied, her voice steady, though her jaw held a trace of rigid tension. "The family council convened in the regional capital to discuss the winter provisioning of the southern estates. As the eldest daughter of the primary line, my seal is required on the grain levies before our deployment orders are sealed."

"Tell your lords to send extra barley to the northern garrisons," Bennett grunted. "The border posts live on sour turnip mash after the first snow."

"I will tell them," she said.

She turned toward Chaitanya.

Chaitanya sat at the edge of the porch, a small bone-handled awl in his hand, re-stitching the frayed leather strap of his boot-gaiter. He laid the awl on the bench and rose to meet her gaze.

The distance between them was five paces, yet the air carried the memory of the fourth practice yard: the white-hot bead of pressure, the frost-rimed clay, and the fierce warmth of her hands clutching his collar.

"Do not let them assign you to an interior garrison," Chaitanya said softly.

Tejaswini’s eyes darkened, the amber flecks within her irises catching the pale afternoon sun. A faint, almost imperceptible smile touched the corner of her lips, quickly masked by the formal composure expected of a high-born daughter of the Realm.

"My uncle tried to purchase an appointment for me in the Capital Provost Guard," she said, her voice dropping so only he could hear. "He claims a daughter of House Varma should not sleep in ditch-water along the Breach."

"And what did you say?"

"I told him the Capital Provost Guard spends their days chasing drunken tax collectors and corrupt merchants," she said, her hand resting on the pommel of her sabre. "And that if he attempted to alter my deployment papers, I would challenge my cousins to an open circle duel in the ancestral courtyard."

Chaitanya inclined his head. "They would lose."

"They would burn," she said simply.

She walked past him toward the waiting carriage. At the carriage door, the liveried driver stepped down to open the gate and take her trunk. Before stepping onto the iron running-board, Tejaswini looked back over her shoulder, her dark eyes locking with Chaitanya’s across the gravel.

"Do not test your forms while I am away," she said, loud enough to sound like a squadmate’s casual warning, yet carrying a cadence of absolute command. "You need someone to pull you out of the ditch."

"I will be here when the horses return," Chaitanya replied.

She stepped into the carriage. The iron door latched with a heavy, hollow thud. With a flick of the driver's whip, the geldings leaned into their collars, the heavy carriage turning in a wide arc before rumbling out through the western iron gate of the Academy.

***

An hour later, the sun dipped behind the jagged spine of the mountains, casting long, purple shadows across the candidate quadrangle.

Bennett walked across the yard from the Commandant’s Administrative Hall, his heavy boots crunching rhythmically on the frosted grass. In his left hand he held a thick roll of parchment sealed with a heavy medallion of red wax.

Anil was still sitting on the armory porch, flipping a coin between his knuckles. "Is that what I think it is?"

Bennett stepped up onto the timber porch, sat down on the central bench, and laid the parchment on the wood between them. With the flat of his thumb, he cracked the red wax seal, revealing the imperial eagle clutching four arrows: the mark of the Expeditionary Vanguard Corps.

Chaitanya leaned forward to look.

The black ink was fresh, written in the formal, rigid cursive of the Ministry of Inscription:

*Vanguard Expeditionary Unit 07*  
*Candidate Strike Roster:*  
*Bennett of the Iron Range: Grade Three Ice/Earth Specialization, Squad Leader.*  
*Tejaswini of House Varma: Grade Two Fire Specialization, Heavy Assault.*  
*Anil of the Lowland Weald: Grade Three Wind Specialization, Forward Reconnaissance.*  
*Chaitanya of the Southern Marches: Grade Three Wind Specialization, Point Guard.*

*Mission Directive:*  
*Unit 07 is formally assigned to the First Field Evaluation Trial. Muster at the Lower Quarry Proving Grounds at the fifth bell of the upcoming rest-day. Full combat gear, live steel, iron rations. The evaluation will be conducted under combat conditions.*

"Live steel," Anil whispered, staring at the wax crumbs on the parchment. "They usually give candidates blunted wooden practice bars for the first trial."

"Not this year," Bennett said, his voice flat as an iron anvil. "The frontier reports speak of movement in the Grey Wastes. The Asura hives are stirring earlier than usual. The Commandant has no interest in coddling candidates who can only fight padded straw."

Chaitanya touched the coarse grain of the parchment with the tip of his finger. The ink was dry, but the pressure of the quill remained pressed into the fibers.

"Who are the proctors?" Chaitanya asked.

Bennett picked up the official envelope and pulled out a secondary slip of grey paper. His brow furrowed as he read the names, his square jaw tightening.

"Proctor Rao," Bennett muttered. "And Proctor Meera."

Anil froze, the coin slipping from between his fingers and dropping onto the pine floorboards with a sharp ring.

"Meera?" Anil’s voice jumped an octave. "The Blue Viper? She broke three candidates' collarbones during the autumn trials two years back. They say her lightning moves faster than an unstrung bow."

"Then we had better ensure our boots are well insulated," Chaitanya said quietly, standing up and looking out toward the dark, shadowed silhouette of the Lower Quarry. "Go to the supply clerk. We need two jars of mutton tallow for the leather, fresh iron points for the darts, and three coils of braided hemp rope."

Bennett looked up at him, a faint grim nod of approval creasing his scarred cheek. "You heard him, Anil. Move. The proctors will not wait for us to finish our mutton."
