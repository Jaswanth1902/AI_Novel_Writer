# Chapter 27: The Broken Bells of Langford

The bronze bell atop the Langford chapel tolled without rhythm, hammered by a terrified sexton whose frantic strokes shook the belfry timber.

Below, the town had descended into raw, muddy chaos. Torches of dipped pine sputtered in the cold drizzle, casting wild orange glare across the churning mud of the main avenue. Five hundred people surged north toward the railway sidings: coal miners with picks slung over their backs, women clutching wrapped infants to their chests, cart wheels snapping in the ruts as overloaded hand-barrows overturned into the ditches.

At the freight siding, the second locomotive stood roaring, its safety valves venting screaming plumes of white steam into the twilight. Lyra stood atop a flatcar with her bow drawn, her raspy voice cutting through the panic:

"Keep moving! Leave the furniture! Children and wounded onto the forward cars! Men with iron tools form on the loading docks!"

A mile to the west, the palisade failed.

It did not break slowly. A fifty-foot section of split-pine timbers detonated inward under the weight of thirty coordinated beasts. Splinters the size of javelins sprayed across the western garden plots, followed immediately by the shrieking vanguard of the Rakshasa kin.

Dozens of grey, bone-crested predators poured through the breach, their three-hinged jaws snapping, claws gouging trenches into the mud as they accelerated toward the fleeing crowd. Behind them loped four tall, gaunt Vetalas, their mimic cries echoing off the timber houses in a terrifying chorus of screaming children.

"Line holds here!" Bennett’s roar rose above the tumult.

The northern cadet stood planted at the intersection where the main thoroughfare narrowed between two three-story stone flour storehouses. He drove his three-foot partisan deep into the cobblestones with both gauntleted hands.

*Earth and frost communion.*

The mud between the stone buildings froze solid in a heartbeat. From the frozen muck reared a seven-foot barrier of jagged, composite ice and packed river rock, angled like the prow of an icebreaker to split the avenue in two.

The leading wave of Rakshasas struck the ice wall at full speed. Bone crests shattered against frozen stone with sickening crunches. Three beasts tumbled over the parapet, their claws scrambling for purchase on the slippery crest.

From the roof of the left-hand storehouse, Anil dropped like a diving hawk.

His palms slammed downward in mid-air, unleashing a concussive air blast that flattened the three climbing beasts back into the street. With his right hand, he loosed two sulfur-greased throwing darts into the eye sockets of a lunging Vetala below, before catching the rain gutter and swinging himself back onto the roof with effortless agility.

"Clear the lane!" Tejaswini shouted from the bed of an overturned timber dray ten paces behind Bennett.

Her dark eyes burned with intense, unyielding light. She drew both hands back to her ribs, her breath locked into the deep, rhythmic three-beat expansion of the southern flame-drives.

She thrust both palms forward.

A roaring sheet of white-hot fire erupted from her hands, filling the fifteen-foot stone corridor between the storehouses from cobblestones to eaves. The heat was so intense the mortar between the stone blocks cracked like drying mud.

The black bile coating the hides of the trapped Rakshasas caught like lamp oil.

The corridor became an incinerator. Explosive pops rattled the leaded glass of the upper windows as the beasts' marrow boiled within their bone crests. Screams of unbearable agony tore from dozens of burning throats, drowned out by the thunderous roar of Tejaswini’s sustained flame-drive.

Within sixty seconds, the vanguard was reduced to piles of smoking, blackened ash and sizzling grease that washed away in the freezing drizzle.

Tejaswini dropped to one knee on the timber dray, her breath tearing in her throat, sweat pouring down her face and evaporating instantly against the residual heat of her skin. Her hands trembled as she gripped the hilt of her sabre for balance.

Bennett leaned heavily on his spear, ice crystals riming his eyebrows and beard, his chest heaving. "Is that... all of them?"

Chaitanya stood five paces ahead of Bennett’s wall, his grey tunic dusted with soot, his boots rooted in the scorched mud.

He did not look at the smoking carcasses. He looked past the burning palisade breach, down into the black timber road leading south into the Oakhaven forest.

"No," Chaitanya said softly.

The chapel bell on the hill stopped tolling.

It did not stop because the sexton had fled. It stopped because the air pressure in the valley dropped by ten millibars in a single second. The heavy bronze clapper swung toward the lip of the bell and froze, held by an unnatural electrostatic vacuum that stilled the wind across the roofs.

The drizzle ceased falling. The raindrops hung suspended in mid-air for a fraction of a heartbeat.

Then the earth beneath Langford lifted two inches.

A deep, subterranean boom tore through the bedrock: the sound of a mountain fault sliding against granite. The three-story stone storehouses groaned, slate shingles sliding from the roofs and smashing on the cobblestones.

From the southern tree line, two hundred paces away, the hundred-year-old pine trees did not bend. They snapped like dry grass.

A shape emerged into the torchlight.

It rose twelve cubits into the misty air: a bipedal juggernaut of black, layered chitin, its massive forelimbs thicker than oak trunks, ending in three-pronged digging spades of solid bone. Its head was a featureless wedge of armor, broken only by a glowing crimson ocular slit that burned with the dull, murderous hatred of the southern Breach.

A Tier Three Danava.

The beast reared onto its hind legs, exhaling a cloud of superheated steam that melted the frost from the roofs for fifty paces.

Its foot came down.

The main street of Langford split open from side to side, and the siege-beast charged.
