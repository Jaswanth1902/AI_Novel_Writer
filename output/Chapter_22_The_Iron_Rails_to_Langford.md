# Chapter 22: The Iron Rails to Langford

The iron wheels of the coal transport screamed against the wet curve of the rails as the train plunged through the Oakhaven Gap.

Outside the narrow slit-windows, the mountains rose in sheer walls of dark basalt, their jagged ledges choked with scrub pine and dripping with grey slush. Coal smoke from the locomotive swirled past the glass in greasy, black plumes, depositing soot along the lead came of the panes. Inside the passenger coach, the small iron stove glowed dull red, smelling of hot iron and burning pine knots.

Anil paced the narrow center aisle, his boots clicking rhythmically against the bolted oak planks. He paused every three paces to bounce on the balls of his feet, adjusting the strap of his dart quiver.

"Sit down, Anil," Bennett said from the corner bench. The squad leader had laid his three-foot partisan across his knees, methodically rubbing a cloth soaked in mutton fat over the iron socket. "You are wearing a trench in the floorboards."

"My legs are stiff," Anil muttered, though he dropped onto the pine bench opposite Bennett. "Two hours of iron clatter and soot. If I wanted to breathe coal smoke, I would have apprenticed to a foundry blacksmith."

"Be glad the rails reach this far south," Tejaswini said without looking away from the window. She sat erect, her dark hair wrapped in a woolen kerchief, her hands folded in her lap over the woolen folds of her mantle. "Thirty years ago, during the pacification campaigns, the imperial regiments marched this defile on foot through six feet of drifts. Two companies froze to death in the gorge before they reached the river plain."

Chaitanya stood near the forward door of the carriage, his right hand resting flat against the iron door frame. Through the vibration of the metal, he tracked the cadence of the engine: eighty strokes a minute on the pistons, the rhythmic surge of high-pressure steam, the slight lateral yaw of the bogies as the train crossed the timber trestle over the Blackwater River.

"The grade is leveling," Chaitanya said.

Ahead, the locomotive blew its whistle twice: short, sharp blasts that signaled the approach to a freight depot.

The train slowed, iron brake blocks groaning against steel tires in a shower of sparks that hissed in the wet gravel. The dark walls of the gorge fell away, opening into a broad, mist-choked valley hemmed in by low hills.

Langford was an ugly town.

Two hundred timber and stone shacks clustered along a muddy river channel where flat-bottomed coal barges sat moored to rotting pilings. Enormous heaps of black bituminous coal towered beside the rail sidings, glistening in the steady mountain drizzle. A low palisade of split pine logs surrounded the southern perimeter, its sharpened stakes reinforced with coils of heavy iron wire.

The train rolled to a complete halt alongside an uncovered wooden freight platform.

When the coach door opened, the air struck them like a wet, freezing rag. It smelled of sulfur, damp slate, horse manure, and river mud.

"Down," Bennett ordered, slinging his canvas pack over both shoulders and stepping onto the iron running board.

The four cadets descended to the platform.

A dozen local men in grease-stained canvas coats and boiled-leather aprons stood under the overhang of the freight shed, watching them in silence. Most held short heavy-hafted timber axes or hunting crossbows with rusted iron prods. Their faces were grey with coal dust and pinched with an exhaustion that ran deeper than bone. None spoke. None offered a greeting. They merely watched the four clean grey uniforms and iron insignia with dull, hollow eyes.

"Not much of a welcoming committee," Anil murmured under his breath, keeping his hand close to his dart bracer.

"They are waiting for soldiers, not students," Tejaswini said quietly.

"Over here, children."

The voice came from the shadow of an overturned baggage cart near the water tower.

A woman stepped out into the drizzle. She was tall, spare, and moved with the silent, fluid grace of a timber cat. She wore a long coat of oiled dark leather, reinforced at the shoulders with overlapping horn scales, and a wide-brimmed hat pulled low over her brow. A short composite recurve bow hung across her back inside a waterproof seal-skin sleeve; at her hips rested three bone-handled skinning knives in stiff rawhide sheaths.

Her face was sharp, tanned by mountain wind, with a pale scar cutting through the arch of her left eyebrow. A stalk of dried winter clover jutted from the corner of her mouth.

She stopped five paces from Bennett, her cold grey eyes sweeping over the four cadets with clinical, unhurried contempt.

"You are Bennett?" she asked.

"Candidate Vanguard Unit Zero-Seven," Bennett said, snapping to attention and producing the iron dispatch tin from his coat. "Assigned under sealed directive to Operative Lyra."

Lyra did not take the tin. She spat the clover stem into the mud between their boots.

"Rao promised me veterans," she said, her voice dry and rasping like shale sliding down a roof. "He promised me soldiers who knew how to put an iron point through an eye socket without throwing up on their boots. Instead, the railway dray dumps four academy pets with unscratched leather and hair pins."

Tejaswini took a half-step forward, her spine stiffening, the air around her collar shimmering with a faint thermal pulse. "Our orders come directly from the Frontier Dispatch, Operative. Our weapons are live steel."

Lyra’s eyes flicked to Tejaswini’s sabre, then to the crimson trim of her travel cloak. A thin, vicious smile touched her lips. "House Varma. A noble girl come to play in the mud. Let us hope the corpse-beasts admire your family crest, Lady Varma, because a Vetala usually begins eating before the prey finishes dying."

Tejaswini’s jaw tightened, but she held her tongue, her discipline overriding her pride.

Lyra turned her gaze to Chaitanya.

Chaitanya stood motionless in the drizzle, his grey tunic dusted with coal soot, his dark eyes meeting hers with absolute stillness. He did not puff out his chest. He did not reach for a weapon. He simply observed the tension in her right shoulder, the slight turn of her hips that positioned her within reach of her skinning knife, and the faint scent of sulfur and wolf-grease clinging to her sleeves.

For a second, the mocking edge in Lyra’s eyes flickered, replaced by a cold, calculating curiosity.

"At least one of you knows how to keep his hands still," she muttered, turning on her heel toward the muddy thoroughfare. "Pick up your gear. The municipal storehouse is half a mile up the river road. We have three hours before dusk, and the night shift in Langford does not sleep with their doors unbarred."
