# Chapter 23: War-Room in the Mist

The municipal grain storehouse was built like a coastal fortress.

Its four-foot walls were dry-stacked granite boulders quarried from the river gorge, bonded with lime mortar that smelled of ancient river shells and damp earth. Inside, the ceiling beams were squared trunks of black spruce, blackened by forty years of wood smoke from the corner hearth. Burlap sacks of winter rye and salt mash stood stacked six high against the western wall, casting heavy shadows across the room.

In the center of the flagstones sat a rough trestle table of split pine, its surface scarred by axe-notches and tallow stains.

Lyra tossed her wide-brimmed oilcloth hat onto a stack of grain sacks and unrolled a sheet of scraped calfskin vellum across the timber. Four heavy iron railroad spikes served as paperweights, pinning the curling edges flat.

On the skin, a map was drawn in charcoal and red iron grease: the river basin of Langford.

"Eat while you listen," Lyra said, gesturing with her chin toward an iron cauldron simmering on the hearth. "You will not see hot food again until tomorrow night."

Anil did not need a second invitation. He ladled boiled barley mash into four cracked earthenware bowls, seasoning them with coarse salt from a wooden trough. The broth was thick, grey, and smelled of wood ash and dried leeks, but it was steaming hot. The four cadets ate standing, their pewter spoons scraping the clay in measured rhythm.

Lyra drew a bone-handled bodkin from her sleeve and tapped the northern corner of the map.

"Site One," she said. "The Old Water Mill on the upper canal. Abandoned twelve years ago when the river silted up. Three days ago, the miller's boy went up the tailrace to check eel traps. He came back with his hair white and his tongue swollen. He says the mill-pond was full of dead carp floating belly-up in greasy red scum. When our militia scout went up to inspect, he found three-foot gouges in the water-wheel casing. The cuts were not made by an iron chisel. They were torn out by claws that secrete black resin."

Tejaswini set her bowl down on the edge of the trestle. "Vetala bile. Master Gopinath’s bestiary lists it as necrotic digestive acid. The corpse-types use it to dissolve marrow before feeding."

Lyra’s grey eyes flicked to Tejaswini with a brief flash of surprise, quickly replaced by her habitual cynicism. "Your academy books are not completely useless, it seems. Yes. Bile."

The point of the bodkin moved down to the western quarter of the town, near the boundary palisade.

"Site Two. The municipal orphanage. Thirty children, mostly miners' orphans and strays from the lower camps. For four consecutive nights, the matron reported scratching inside the stone floor drains. At first she blamed river rats. Then two night watchmen heard a child crying in the ditch outside the palisade. When they opened the wicket gate with lanterns, the ditch was empty, but both men smelled rotting pork."

"Sound mimicry," Bennett said, his brow creasing into a deep northern scowl. "The Vetala uses human distress calls to pull sentries out of fortified enclosures."

"And Site Three," Lyra said, driving the point of the iron bodkin half an inch into the pine table at the southern hills. "Pit Four. The deepest coal shaft in the valley, descending four hundred cubits into the mountain fault. The miners struck a natural cavern two weeks back. Yesterday, the morning shift found their pit-props smashed like toothpicks. Two timber mules were found dead in the haulage tunnel: ribs crushed flat, carcasses untouched, spines shattered by blunt impact."

Silence fell over the room. The only sound was the crackle of a pine knot in the hearth and the muffled thud of rain on the slate roof.

Chaitanya laid his spoon in his empty bowl and stepped up to the table. He studied the three red grease marks, his gaze traveling between the elevations of the valley floor.

"These are not three separate packs," Chaitanya said quietly.

Lyra looked at him, one eyebrow cocked. "Go on, scholar."

"The water mill is at the highest elevation along the northern canal," Chaitanya explained, his finger tracing the blue charcoal line of the river. "The orphanage sits directly over the old Roman drainage conduit that discharges into the lower tailrace. And Pit Four cuts through the southern limestone aquifer that feeds both."

He looked up, meeting Lyra’s hard gaze. "The creatures are using the underground hydrological channels to navigate beneath the town without crossing the open streets. They are establishing perimeter markers."

Bennett’s hand dropped to his spear socket. "A reconnaissance screen."

"Worse," Chaitanya said. "A vanguard. Gopinath told us that corpse-types do not operate with strategic coordination unless an apex predator is driving them from behind. They are hollowing out our escape routes. What crushed the timber mules in Pit Four was not a Vetala."

"No," Lyra murmured, the sarcasm entirely vanished from her voice. Her knuckles whitened on the table. "A Danava. A Tier Three siege-beast. Its mass alone would shatter the palisades."

She pulled the bodkin from the wood and tucked it back into her rawhide sleeve.

"We move in twenty minutes," Lyra commanded, looking at each of them in turn. "Bennett, you take the southern gate; verify the militia has sufficient sulfur oil and ensure the iron portcullis can drop without jamming. Anil, take the church steeple; get above the mist and watch the northern timber line for tree movement. Tejaswini, Chaitanya, you come with me to the Old Water Mill. We verify the bile traces and burn the nest before dusk."

Tejaswini picked up her sabre, buckling the sword-belt tight around her waist. "And if the nest is occupied?"

Lyra pulled her oiled wide-brimmed hat back over her eyes, a vicious, cold smile touching her lips.

"Then we see if your fire burns hot enough to cleanse the rot, Lady Varma."
