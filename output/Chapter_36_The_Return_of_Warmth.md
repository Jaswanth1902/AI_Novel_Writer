# Chapter 36: The Return of Warmth

By the beginning of the next week, the rhythm of Quarters Seven had returned to life.

It slipped back into the room so gradually that none of them marked the exact hour: through laughter breaking out over burnt flatbread, conversations overlapping across the pine table, and the steady, reassuring clatter of boots being kicked off by the door.

At the fourth bell of dawn, Chaitanya stood on the cedar roof above the third-tier dormitory.

The valley below was submerged in a sea of white river mist, the mountain peaks jutting above the fog like black islands in an arctic ocean. Chaitanya wore only his thin grey linen trousers, his bare torso exposed to the sub-zero wind.

Across his left shoulder, the pale line of the healed fracture showed only as a faint, silver seam against the brown skin. His breathing was silent. With every pivot of his hips, he extended a hand into the freezing mist, not pushing the air, but drawing the density inward. The fog condensed around his forearms in tight, spiraling whorls, freezing into microscopic frost needles that drifted to the roof shingles without a sound.

When the bell struck five, he pulled on his wool tunic and descended the wooden loft ladder back into the warm quarters.

The room smelled of toasted rye flatbread, wood smoke, and roasted chicory brew.

On the iron stove, a flat griddle of cast iron held four thick rounds of dark bread, crisping to a dark golden brown over the birch coals. Tejaswini stood by the stove, a wooden spatula in her hand, wearing a simple grey woolen tunic with the sleeves rolled above her elbows. Her hair was bound in a single braid that fell over her left shoulder, pinned with a plain boxwood needle.

At the table, Bennett sat with a three-foot length of seasoned mountain ash held between his knees, drawing a piece of rough sharkskin along the wood. Pale shavings of ash curled onto the floorboards around his boots, smelling of fresh sap and bitter tannin. Beside him lay his original partisan head, cleaned of Langford muck and polished until the bevel caught the lantern glare like a mirror.

Anil was sprawled across the corner bench, his mouth full of bread crust, gesturing wildly with a newly feathered throwing dart.

"I am telling you, Bennett," Anil said around his mouthful, "the wind-corridor off the south bleachers creates a four-inch drift at fifty paces. If you throw for the center of the chest, the bolt will strike the shoulder plate every single time. You have to aim for the left hip."

"If you aim for the hip," Bennett replied without looking up from his sanding, "and the target pivots on his back foot, your dart catches empty air. Aim for the mass. The wind takes an inch, the iron still takes the meat."

Chaitanya pulled up a wooden stool and sat at the corner of the table.

Tejaswini turned from the stove, slid a hot, smoking round of rye bread onto a wooden trencher, and set it before him. Beside the bread, she placed a cracked earthenware cup filled with steaming chicory brew, dark and bitter as pine bark.

"Eat before Anil steals the crust," she said, leaning her hip against the edge of the table, a faint, teasing smile curving the corner of her lips.

Chaitanya broke the hot bread with his fingers. Steam rose from the coarse crumb, carrying the rich, nutty smell of roasted caraway. "The haft is thicker than the old one, Bennett."

Bennett paused with his sharkskin, tapping the ash wood with his thumb. "An inch and a half at the grip, tapered to an inch at the socket. Rao’s armorer gave me the timber from the reserve artillery stock. It won't snap under five tons of direct impact."

"And the ribs?" Chaitanya asked, meeting the northern cadet’s eyes.

Bennett rolled his left shoulder in a slow, full circle. A faint pop sounded in the cartilage, but his face remained steady, untouched by pain. "The bone is hard enough to take a practice stave. I won't be diving off walls, but I can hold the sand."

Anil leaned across the table, snatching a scrap of warm bread from the trencher with a triumphant grin before Tejaswini could swat his fingers with her spatula.

"The brackets are being posted at noon," Anil said, his eyes bright with excitement. "Thirty-two candidate units. The senior squads have been strutting through the quadrangle like peacocks in new armor. Anirudh’s boys took the center table in the mess hall this morning, boasting that they would finish every preliminary match within ninety seconds."

Tejaswini poured herself a cup of the hot chicory, the steam rising past her lashes. "Let them boast. Pride makes a man heavy on his heels. When the sand moves beneath his boots, an arrogant bender falls twice as hard."

She turned her gaze to Chaitanya.

The room grew momentarily quiet, the companionable banter settling into that deep, silent solidarity that bound the four of them together. Through the open window embrasure, the morning sun broke over the mountain ridge, sending a shaft of warm gold across the pine table, illuminating the polished iron of Bennett’s spearhead and the dark grain of the wood.

Chaitanya drank his chicory, the bitter warmth spreading through his chest, anchoring the newly knit meridians into his marrow.

"We do not fight to finish matches in ninety seconds," Chaitanya said quietly, looking at each of them in turn. "We fight to control the sand from the first heartbeat to the last. When Unit Seven steps into the ring, we leave no doubt."

Bennett drove the butt of his ash haft into the floorboards with a dull, solid *thud* that shook the trencher.

"No doubt," Bennett agreed, his grin wide and dangerous. "Now eat your bread, point guard. We have three hours of tactical forms before the noon heralds."
