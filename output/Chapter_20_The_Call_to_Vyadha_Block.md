# Chapter 20: The Call to Vyadha Block

Rao looked down at the hand pressed against his sternum.

He looked at Chaitanya’s calm, unblinking eyes, then shifted his gaze to the glowing edge of Tejaswini’s sabre resting an inch from his throat, and finally to Bennett’s spear-point wedged into his shoulder joint.

A single bead of sweat rolled through the grey dust caked on the veteran’s temple.

Then his chest expanded in a deep, rumbling rattle, and he barked out a laugh that dislodged chunks of fractured basalt from his sleeve.

"That is enough," Rao boomed, raising his open palms. "Stand down, cubs. Before you set my beard on fire."

Bennett lowered his partisan first, his shoulders dropping two inches as he exhaled a ragged, trembling breath. Tejaswini held her blade steady for one more heartbeat, her amber eyes burning, before she stepped back, flicked the glowing residue of flame from the steel with a snap of her wrist, and slid the sabre into its scabbard. The iron clasp locked with a clean, dry click.

Chaitanya drew back his hand. His fingers were cold, his pulse already slowing to sixty beats a minute.

Across the perimeter, Proctor Meera clicked the ends of her twin copper batons together. The high-voltage hum died instantly, leaving only the sharp tang of burnt ozone hanging over the pit. She slipped the batons into the leather sheaths at her thighs with practiced ease, her pale eyes fixed entirely on Chaitanya.

Anil collapsed where he stood on the scree slope, his limbs spreading out like a starfish against the shattered limestone. His tunic was torn at the shoulder, soaked through with sweat and powdered lime.

"My ribs," Anil wheezed toward the overcast sky. "Tell the kitchen proctors to bury me with a bowl of salted lard. I am never moving again."

Bennett walked stiffly to a quarried limestone block and sat down, leaning his spear against his knee. He reached to his belt, unhooked a heavy pigskin water flask, pulled the wooden stopper with his teeth, and took three long swallows. He passed the flask to Tejaswini without speaking.

Tejaswini took the flask with soot-blackened fingers. She drank deeply, water spilling over the corner of her chin and running down her neck into the collar of her tunic. When she handed the leather flask to Chaitanya, her knuckles brushed his.

"Your hands are like ice," she murmured, her voice rough from smoke.

"The vacuum draws thermal energy from the skin," Chaitanya said quietly, taking the flask and rinsing his mouth before swallowing. The well-water tasted of iron and cold clay, cutting through the sulfur coating his throat.

Proctor Rao stripped off his broken basalt gauntlet, tossing the shattered fragments into the scree with a heavy clatter. He walked over to the squad, his boots crunching in the rubble. Up close, the scars on his neck and forearms were thick, pale cords carved by claws that had never belonged to any beast of the inner provinces.

"Your line was ragged," Rao said, pointing a thick, blunt finger at Anil on the slope. "You leaped off the wall without securing your footing, boy. A Danava with half a brain would have swatted you out of the air like a fat gnat. If you scout, you keep two points of retreat open at all times."

Anil managed a faint, sheepish nod from the dirt, keeping his eyes shut.

Rao turned his heavy gaze to Bennett. "Your ice palisade was solid. But you anchored it only four feet into the bedrock. Against an Asura earth-spike, the whole wall would have tipped backward and crushed your own squad. Dig deeper next time. Use the water to crack the stone beneath the surface before you freeze it."

"Understood, Proctor," Bennett answered, dipping his head.

Then Rao looked at Tejaswini. A faint, grudging respect softened the hard corners of his mouth. "House Varma still breeds warriors, Lady Tejaswini. That thermal shield was fast. Without it, Meera’s arc would have taken your spearman’s throat. Your father would not be ashamed."

"Thank you, Proctor Rao," she replied, her posture returning to that rigid, aristocratic dignity, though her knees were trembling beneath her woolens.

Meera approached the group. She walked without sound, her boots leaving no deeper impression in the gravel than dry leaves. She stopped three feet from Chaitanya, folding her lean arms across the steel mesh of her chest.

"You," Meera said. Her voice had the flat, dangerous clarity of an unstrung bow.

Chaitanya looked up from his water flask, his expression neutral. "Proctor."

"I have fought sixty skirmishes along the southern trenches," she said, tilting her head. "I have fought rogue lightning casters, foreign raiders, and thunder-serpents. When lightning leaves the baton, it seeks the lowest resistance to ground. It does not turn forty-five degrees in mid-air to hit an iron survey pin unless someone pulls the air itself out of the way."

The silence in the quarry grew dense. Tejaswini’s hand shifted an inch toward the hilt of her sabre, her knuckles white. Bennett looked up from his spear, his brow furrowing with sudden caution.

"The air in the pit was saturated with copper dust from your batons," Chaitanya said, his voice flat, steady, and devoid of tremor. "When the proctor threw the boulder, the impact shattered the lime rock, creating a cloud of calcium carbonate particles. A high-density wind shear along the right flank ionized the calcium dust, creating a low-impedance channel that guided the charge toward the iron survey rod."

Meera stared at him. Her grey eyes were cold, calculating every syllable, weighing the physics against her own instincts.

"A cadet who calculates atmospheric ionization gradients while an eight-foot bolt is coming at his chest," she whispered, her lips curling into a sharp, humorless slant. "Either you are the most frighteningly calm tactician this academy has produced in twenty years, or you are playing with things that do not belong to the Four Gates."

"I only know what Master Varun wrote in his treatises on wind displacement," Chaitanya replied calmly.

Meera held his gaze for five long seconds. Then she let out a short, dry breath and uncrossed her arms.

"We shall see," she said. "The border tests theories very quickly."

Rao reached into the heavy leather pack slung over his shoulder and pulled out an iron cylinder six inches long, wrapped in oiled black canvas. The end was sealed with a thick stamp of black pitch, bearing the imprint of a wolf’s head impaled upon an iron trident: the mark of the Vyadha Block.

Bennett straightened up on his rock, his breath catching. Even Anil opened his eyes, sitting up on his elbows in the scree.

"You are not assigned to the grain patrols in the west," Rao said, holding the cylinder out. "And you are not guarding tax wagons along the river road. Those duties are for cadets who need two years to learn how to hold a spear."

He placed the cold iron cylinder into Bennett’s waiting hands.

"The Vyadha Block?" Bennett’s voice cracked slightly. "That is the outermost fortress on the southern fault line. The Redoubts."

"The scouts reported three breaches along the Fourth Trench yesterday morning," Rao said, his face darkening like thunder over the plains. "Corpse-packs have pushed past the palisades. The garrisons are burning their dead before dusk to keep them from turning. The Commandant needs fresh blood who can fight without screaming."

Rao turned toward the northern road, his heavy basalt armor creaking with every stride.

"Pack your iron rations tonight," he called over his shoulder. "Sharpen your steel until it shaves arm-hair. The military transport wagons leave the main gate at the fourth bell of dawn. If you are late, you can walk sixty miles through the mud alone."

Meera cast one last, unreadable glance at Chaitanya, then turned and followed the giant into the evening mist.

The four cadets remained in the center of the quarry.

The sun had sunk beneath the western ridges, leaving the sky the color of cold bruise-purple. Down in the valley, the first warning lanterns were being lit along the high palisade walls of the Academy, tiny sparks of yellow light against the rising dark.

To the south, beyond the ragged teeth of the limestone peaks, the horizon hung black and endless: the vast, untamed expanse of the Breach, where the Old Empire had fallen and the beasts waited in the fog.

Tejaswini stepped up beside Chaitanya, her shoulder touching his. Her breath plumed in the twilight, warm and steady.

"The Vyadha Block," she whispered.

Chaitanya looked out into the southern darkness, his hand resting on the rough hilt of his hunting knife.

"Good," he said softly. "The further we are from the capital, the less they will be watching."
