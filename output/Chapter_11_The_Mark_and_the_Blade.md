# Chapter 11: The Mark and the Blade

The limestone gravel inside the chalk square was stained with old brine and rust.

Anil stood on the intersection of the two white lines, his broad shoulders squared, his bare forearms damp with cold sweat despite the freezing mountain draft. His practice trousers, rolled rough above his ankles, flapped faintly in the eddy curling off his boots.

Ten paces away, two imperial assistants in heavy leather arm-guards stepped forward, three forged iron kunai fanned between their gauntleted fingers.

"Candidate Eighty-Eight," Senior Proctor Rann called out from behind his pine table. "Phase One. Interception."

Rann’s gauntlet dropped.

The first iron blade came fast: a flat, level throw aimed straight at the hollow of Anil’s chest.

Anil did not flinch. His knees dipped two inches, his weight driving deep into his heels, and his palms snapped forward in a short, upward arc. The air between him and the assistant compressed with the dull, concussive *whump* of a wet blanket shaken in the wind. 

The kunai met the invisible wall four feet from his breast. The heavy steel shuddered, lost its spin, and hovered for a fraction of a second before tumbling nose-down into the gravel.

The second and third blades followed in unbroken succession. Anil grunted, his teeth bared, driving his breath down into his lower abdomen. Two separate pressure pockets formed in front of his ribs. Both blades struck the boundary, their kinetic momentum arrested with sharp, metallic vibrations, dropping harmlessly beside the first.

"Clear," Rann barked, making an unceremonious mark on his slate. "Phase Two. Directed Strike."

An assistant laid a clean, ungreased kunai on a flat wooden peg at Anil’s feet. Fifty paces distant, across the barren expanse of the pit, three thick pine roundels hung from iron chains.

Anil drew a long, ragged breath through his nose. He extended his right hand, fingers curled, lifting the weapon with a steady, rising draft. The iron rose to eye level, wobbling slightly along its horizontal axis as the boy struggled with the aerodynamic balance.

With a sharp forward thrust of his palm, Anil drove the air current forward.

The blade whistled across the gravel. *Thunk.* It buried itself three inches deep into the outer white ring of the center target. 

The second throw followed, landing four inches lower, biting hard into the pine bark. The third strike, corrected with a fierce snap of his wrist, struck dead center into the painted bullseye, sending splinters flying into the dirt.

A low murmur of surprise rippled through the novices waiting along the perimeter ropes.

"Phase Three," Rann commanded, his scarred face showing no pleasure. "Multi-Stream Control."

Three dry reed-paper spheres, light as sparrows, were placed on three low pegs: one to his left, one forward, and one to his right.

"Ten seconds," the timekeeper announced, lifting an iron hour-candle marked with lead bells. "Begin."

Anil widened his stance. His jaw set hard. His arms spread outward, palms turned down toward the gravel, while a third vector of focused air rose directly from the crown of his head. 

The three spheres lifted simultaneously. 

They did not sit in calm water. They bobbed erratically, caught in the competing downdrafts of the valley wind, drifting within inches of the ground before Anil’s twitching fingers corrected the flow. His neck veins corded with strain; a drop of sweat ran down the bridge of his nose, freezing on his lip.

*Nine. Ten.*

A small lead bell dropped into the bronze basin with a sharp chime.

"Clear," Rann said, his pen scratching across the vellum ledger. "Phase Four. Sustained Defense."

At the far end of the station, the blackened tree stump stood in the center of the five-foot chalk circle. An assistant placed the inflated pig-bladder balloon gently on the wood. It was translucent, yellowed, and thin enough that a coarse thorn would have burst it.

Anil stepped inside the circle. His practice shirt was dark with sweat across the spine.

"The rules remain absolute," Rann reminded him, his eyes cold and unblinking. "Ten seconds. If the balloon touches the stump, leaves the chalk cylinder, or if steel touches your skin, your registration is terminated."

Anil raised his hand. A gentle, curling cushion of air lifted the balloon six inches above the flat stump. The thin membrane hovered there, trembling in the cold air like a bubble of oil.

"Begin."

Two assistants stepped forward, their gauntlets loaded with unpolished steel.

The first kunai flew straight for the balloon.

Anil pivoted on his heel, keeping both boots inside the white circle, and snapped his left elbow upward. A narrow jet of air deflected the incoming blade, sending it clattering away into the gravel. But the sudden diversion of airflow pulled the balloon two inches to the left, dangerously close to the chalk border.

Anil gasped, sweeping his right palm in a panic to pull the bladder back toward the center.

At that instant, the second assistant threw low, aiming directly at Anil’s left thigh.

There was no time to think. Anil twisted his hip, pulling his leg back, while maintaining the overhead draft with his raised hand. The blade whistled past his thigh, its sharp bevel slicing cleanly through the coarse fabric of his trousers, grazing the skin without drawing blood, before burying itself in the earth.

The balloon wobbled violently, dropping toward the wood.

Anil dropped to one knee, driving both palms upward with a desperate, guttural roar. A final gust caught the bladder half an inch above the bark, holding it suspended as the lead bell struck the bronze basin.

*Clang.*

Silence fell over the pit.

Anil remained on one knee, his chest heaving with violent, shuddering gasps, staring down at the torn hem of his trousers.

Rann looked at the candidate for three seconds, then turned to his ledger.

"Candidate Eighty-Eight," the proctor droned. "Passed. Collect your certified slip at the desk."

Anil exhaled a breath that sounded like a sob. He pushed himself up from the dirt, his legs shaking faintly, and turned to look across the perimeter ropes.

Chaitanya stood there, his long grey sleeves hanging still, meeting his friend's bloodshot eyes with a slow, proud nod.

Anil wiped the sweat from his jaw with the back of his arm, managed a trembling grin, and stepped off the chalk.

"Next," Rann shouted into the gathering frost. "Candidate Ninety. Chaitanya of the South Wing. Take the mark."

***

Chaitanya walked onto the chalk cross with the unhurried composure of a scholar taking his seat in an empty library.

He wore no arm-guards, no heavy leather training boots. His plain linen practice tunic hung straight from his shoulders, and around his left wrist rested the band of dull iron, cold and unmoving against his pulse.

The two assistants faced him, their eyes hardened by fifty previous throws.

"Phase One," Rann said, his voice flat. "Begin."

The three kunai were released in a tight, overlapping volley: one high for the throat, two level for the chest. They flew with lethal precision, fifty paces per second, iron whistling against the frost.

Chaitanya did not raise his arms. He did not sink into a horse stance.

He simply exhaled through his nostrils, his left fingers curling slightly inward.

Two feet in front of his chest, the atmospheric density of the air changed. It was not a wall; it was a localized field of hyper-compressed air molecules forming a transparent pressure pillow.

The three iron blades struck the field.

There was no sound of impact. The weapons did not bounce, clatter, or wobble. 

They simply stopped.

All three kunai remained suspended in mid-air, perfectly horizontal, locked in the frozen density of the pocket like flies trapped in clear amber.

A dead, eerie silence settled over the entire testing ground.

The assistants froze, their empty hands still extended. Along the perimeter ropes, the murmuring novices went utterly still.

Chaitanya let his fingers relax.

The pressure pocket dissolved. The three kunai dropped simultaneously, striking the gravel nose-first in a neat, straight row three inches before his toes.

Rann stared at the blades. His quill hovered above the slate, motionless for two full seconds.

"Phase Two," the proctor said slowly, his voice dropping into a dangerous, assessing register. "Directed Strike."

An assistant, visibly shaken, set a kunai on the peg.

Chaitanya did not wait for an order. His hand rose six inches. The blade lifted without a tremor, perfectly parallel to the earth, its unpolished edge catching the dull winter sunlight.

He did not push with wind. He evacuated the air in front of the target, creating a zero-resistance vacuum channel fifty paces long.

*Whist.*

The sound was not an iron throw; it was the high-pitched hum of a needle through silk.

The kunai shot across the arena with terrifying speed. It did not hit the outer ring. It drove dead into the center of the bullseye with enough kinetic force to bury the blade to its copper hilt, splitting the three-inch pine roundel clean down its grain.

Before the wood stopped vibrating, Chaitanya lifted the second and third blades, sending them through the same channel. Both struck the exact wound made by the first, driving the iron through the target until the backing stones rang with the impact.

Rann rose slightly from his cedar bench, his grey brows knit in deep suspicion. "Phase Three. Multi-Stream."

The three reed-paper spheres sat upon their pegs.

Chaitanya didn't move his arms. He held his palms flat against his thighs.

The three spheres rose simultaneously to the exact height of his chin. 

For ten seconds, the mountain gale tore across the limestone pit, howling through the ropes and whipping the banners above the pavilions. Yet the three spheres remained completely stationary: not a millimeter of drift, not a fraction of a wobble, held in three independent micro-vortices as rigid as columns of carved ice.

*Clang.*

The lead bell struck. Chaitanya lowered his awareness, and the three spheres settled back onto their wooden pegs without touching the gravel.

Rann walked to the edge of Station Four himself, his heavy iron boots grinding into the chalk.

The blackened stump was cleared. The yellowed pig-bladder was placed upon the wood.

"Candidate Ninety," Rann said, his voice hard as flint, stepping two paces from the circle. "Phase Four. Ten seconds. Assistants, throw to eliminate."

Chaitanya stepped inside the white chalk boundary. 

He looked at the fragile membrane of the balloon. He did not generate an upward draft beneath it. Instead, he enclosed the stump and the balloon within a continuous, self-circulating cylinder of laminar air, sealing the interior volume from the surrounding gale.

The balloon floated three inches above the wood, motionless.

"Begin!"

Four kunai were hurled at once from two directions: two aimed at the boy’s ribs, two aimed straight at the fragile bladder.

Chaitanya did not duck. He did not move his feet.

He simply rotated his wrists, altering the exterior shear angle of the air cylinder.

The four blades entered the boundary layer. 

The moment the steel crossed the threshold, the aerodynamic drag multiplied tenfold. The blades lost their forward momentum instantly, their tips pitching downward, and tumbled into the dirt beside his boots like dropped firewood.

Inside the cylinder, the balloon never wavered. It sat upon its cushion of calm air, as serene as an egg in a nest.

*Clang.*

The timekeeper’s bell chimed for the final time.

Chaitanya stepped out of the chalk circle, his breathing steady, his grey tunic smooth and uncreased, not a single drop of sweat upon his brow.

He walked to the proctor’s desk and stood waiting.

Proctor Rann looked down at the candidate’s slip, then up at Chaitanya’s dark, unreadable eyes. The veteran had evaluated five thousand disciples over twenty years of frontier service, and he knew the signature of raw muscle. This was not muscle. This was something ancient, calculated, and unnervingly precise.

Rann dipped his brass seal into the crimson cinnabar paste.

With a heavy, deliberate thud, the seal struck the yellow vellum.

"Candidate Ninety," Rann said quietly, the flat hostility in his voice replaced by something far more cautious. "Passed with distinction. Report to the central pavilion for registration."

Chaitanya took the slip, bowed with clean, unhurried courtesy, and turned away from the mark.

At the edge of the pit, Anil was waiting, his coat slung over his bruised shoulder, grinning through his exhaustion. Across the grounds, through the thinning crowds of the afternoon, Chaitanya could see the red mantle of Tejaswini and the pale blue coat of Bennett already emerging from their respective arenas, both clutching stamped certificates of their own.

All four were through.

The empire had demanded soldiers, and the four of them had answered the call without breaking their circle.
