# Chapter 26: The Cavern of the Ridge

The trail climbed the spine of the Oakhaven Ridge through thickets of black spruce and rotting deadfall.

Rain had turned to freezing sleet as the elevation rose five hundred cubits above the valley floor. Pine needles crackled like dry glass beneath the iron rims of their boots. The wind blew from the southern waste, carrying the sharp, caustic stench of sulfur fumaroles and something heavier: the greasy, unwashed odor of an enormous meat-eater’s den.

Lyra led the column, her composite bow held across her chest inside its rawhide sleeve, her boots leaving almost no impression on the frosted moss. Behind her came Bennett, his three-foot partisan held at low ready, followed by Tejaswini and Chaitanya. Fifty paces to their right, Anil moved through the lower branches of the spruce trees, vaulting from bough to bough with silent, feather-light updrafts.

At the crest of the ridge, the forest ended abruptly against a sheer amphitheater of black basalt.

The mouth of the cavern yawned twenty paces across.

It was not a natural fissure carved by water. The stone around the portal had been sheared away in violent, stepped gouges, as though an enormous siege ram had pulverized the living rock. Boulders the size of timber wagons lay scattered across the talus slope, cracked into jagged shards by concussive blunt impact.

Lyra crouched behind a moss-covered boulder, waving the squad down.

"Look at the lip," she whispered, her voice rough with cold.

Chaitanya moved past her without a sound, crouching at the eastern rim of the cavern mouth.

On the scree lay a curved plate of dark, iridescent material, four feet long and three fingers thick. It resembled the shell of a colossal river beetle, but heavier, dense as forged pig iron. Along its inner surface clung dried strips of yellow connective tissue that reeked of ammonia and burnt fat.

Bennett knelt beside him, running the iron tip of his spear along the rim of the shell. The metal screeched against the chitin, producing a bright shower of orange sparks without leaving so much as a scratch on the black surface.

"Chitin armor," Bennett muttered, his jaw hardening into stone. "Master Gopinath wasn't exaggerating. Iron points will not pierce this from the front. You need a siege ballista or a direct thermal fracture to break the plates."

Chaitanya picked up a handful of crushed shale from the threshold. The rock was pulverized into powder fine as wheat flour, mixed with pools of black, congealed bile.

"Mass approximately twelve to fifteen tons," Chaitanya said, measuring the stride distance between the three-toed indentations pressed into the frozen mud. "Stride length five paces. It moves with a quadrupedal gallop when accelerating, but rears on two legs to strike downward. The impact force exceeds fifty thousand pounds per square inch."

A sharp, bird-like whistle sounded from the canopy of a tall hemlock forty paces down the slope.

Anil dropped from the lower branches, landing in a low crouch beside Tejaswini. His breath came in ragged, white plumes; his tunic was dusted with snow, his face pale beneath the grime.

"The northern timber line," Anil whispered, pointing back toward the valley with a trembling iron dart. "They are moving. Not one or two scouts. Dozens of them. Rakshasa packs running through the creek beds, and five corpse-types herding them from the rear."

Tejaswini stood up, her hand closing tightly around the wire-wound grip of her sabre. Her eyes were fixed on the valley below, where the first faint curls of smoke were rising from the chimneys of Langford. "They are timing the assault for dusk."

"They are not assaulting the town to sack it," Chaitanya said, rising from the talus. His dark eyes swept over the valley layout: the river, the narrow bridge, the rail siding. "They are herding. The corpse-packs will strike the western palisade to force the population toward the northern gate, where the river narrows into a gorge. The Danava will wait at the bridge to crush the crowd in the bottleneck."

Lyra’s knuckles whitened on her bow. "Five hundred miners and sixty children. If they reach the river gorge in the dark, it will be a slaughterhouse."

"Then we make sure the evacuation moves by the iron rails instead of the river road," Bennett said, slamming the butt of his spear into the scree. "Anil, run ahead. Tell the station master to fire up the second freight locomotive. Hook every flatcar in the yard to the tender."

"And the town?" Tejaswini asked, looking at Chaitanya.

"We hold the main thoroughfare," Chaitanya replied, his voice calm, flat, and chillingly steady against the mountain wind. "Bennett on the central ice palisade. Tejaswini on thermal suppression along the alleys. Lyra covers the rail platform."

Lyra looked at Chaitanya, her grey eyes hard and solemn beneath the brim of her hat. "And you, cadet?"

Chaitanya looked down into the darkening valley, where the first horn was already beginning to sound the alarm from the church tower.

"I take the point," he said. "When the Danava comes for the bridge, I meet it first."
