# Chapter 21: The Despatch of Vyadha Block

The fourth bell of dawn had not finished echoing across the snow-dusted timber roofs of the Academy when Unit 07 reached the gates of the Vyadha Block.

The building was a low, squat redoubt constructed of split pine logs and mortar-less river boulders, built directly into the southern perimeter palisade. Snow clung to the cedar shingles, dripping in slow, freezing beads onto the gravel below. Above the doorway hung the black iron placard of the Frontier Dispatch: an iron wolf’s head pierced through the jaws by a three-pronged spear.

Inside, the air carried the bitter, sharp reek of burning tobacco leaves, wet wool, and stale ink.

A single tallow lantern swung from a ceiling beam, casting long, erratic shadows across rows of cedar pigeonholes packed with rolled manifests. Behind a narrow counter of scarred oak sat Warrant Officer Kaelan. He was a man in his late forties whose right ear had been torn off during the second winter campaign, leaving a knotted puckering of scar tissue against his jaw. A steel dip-pen scratched rhythmically against a ledger of grey rag paper.

Bennett halted two paces from the counter, his heels coming together with an iron click. "Vanguard Strike Unit Zero-Seven reporting for muster, Warrant Officer."

Kaelan did not look up immediately. He finished an entry, blew on the wet ink, and laid the steel pen across the lip of a ceramic inkpot. His one good ear twitched slightly as he turned his rheumy gaze onto the four cadets.

"You are early," Kaelan said, his voice flat as an iron spade striking frozen turf. "Rao’s candidates are usually still in the infirmary having their bruised ribs wrapped in vinegar-bandages."

Bennett reached into the breast pocket of his heavy wool coat and placed the black-wax cylinder onto the oak counter.

Kaelan’s hand paused over the wood. His yellowed fingernails, chipped and stained with tobacco juice, touched the black pitch seal. His eyebrows climbed half an inch. With the point of a blunt desk knife, he sliced through the linen thread, cracked the pitch, and shook the rolled vellum onto his ledger.

The office fell dead silent, save for the rhythmic hiss of a pot of chicory water simmering over the iron brazier in the corner.

Kaelan read the lines twice. His scarred neck tightened, the tendons standing out like corded rope beneath his leather collar.

"Langford," he said at last, resting the back of his knife against his chin. "The mining railhead at the mouth of the Oakhaven Gap."

Anil shifted his weight from one boot to the other, his iron dart bracers clicking against his hip. "Is that an active combat zone?"

Kaelan gave a short, humorless snort that ended in a rattling chest cough. He spat a dark wad of tobacco into a brass sawdust box beside his stool. "An active combat zone? Boy, Langford is sixty miles south of the garrison lines. It is a ditch filled with coal miners, timber rafts, and five hundred civilians who think the Imperial Army exists to shovel snow off their porches."

"Then why does Proctor Rao send an entire combat vanguard under sealed orders?" Tejaswini asked, her voice calm and level, though her hand rested firmly on the hilt of her sabre.

Kaelan picked up a pair of iron tongs, turned a glowing coal in the brazier, and turned his gaze back to them.

"Because three days ago, the water in the Langford mill-pond turned the color of stagnant sheep’s blood," Kaelan said quietly. "And because the night shift at Pit Four reported something moving in the ventilation adits: something that mimics the sound of a crying infant and leaves claw gouges four inches deep in green oak timber."

A brief chill passed through the small room, thick with the weight of Gopinath’s anatomical lectures.

*Tier Two corpse-types,* Chaitanya thought, his pulse remaining steady and slow. *Vetala.*

"The town alderman requested a militia squad," Kaelan continued, pulling four rectangular iron transit tokens from a wooden drawer beneath the counter. He slapped them onto the ledger. Each token was stamped with the double-crossed picks of the Imperial Mining Directorate and the running hound of the military railway. "The Commandant is sending you instead. You ride the south-bound coal transport from Yard Three. Departure is at the fifth bell."

He pulled out a logistics ledger and turned it toward Bennett. "Sign your names, squad leader. Four sets of winter cloaks, four rations tins of salted horsemeat, four flasks of pine-needle tincture. If you die in the shafts, your gear remains the property of the Seventh Division."

Bennett took the steel pen, dipped it into the black gall ink, and signed his name with firm, blocky strokes. Tejaswini signed next, her script elegant and razor-sharp, followed by Anil’s hurried scrawl and Chaitanya’s balanced, unhurried hand.

Kaelan gathered the vellum orders and stuffed them into an iron dispatch tin.

"Your contact in Langford is an operative named Lyra," Kaelan said, sliding the four iron tokens across the counter. "She runs the regional observation post out of the freight depot. She does not like academy cadets. She likes cadets who get in her way even less. Do not challenge her authority unless you want a skinning knife stuck into the heel of your boot."

"We understand, Warrant Officer," Bennett said, pocketing the tokens.

Chaitanya hoisted his canvas pack onto his shoulder, the coarse hemp straps biting into his wool coat. "What about ammunition requisitions?"

Kaelan gestured with his knife toward the rear door leading into the yard. "Quartermaster’s bay on the left. Two boxes of live sulfur flares, four iron-tipped throwing harpoons, and twenty bundles of dried birch torches. Take what you can carry on your backs. The coal train does not wait for pack mules."

***

The morning air in Yard Three was thick with white steam and the acrid, sulfurous stench of bituminous coal.

The locomotive was a towering, riveted brute of blackened iron, its high boiler clad in bands of burnished steel. Deep within its belly, the firebox roared with an orange fury, the draft fed by the rhythmic, mechanical pulse of its steam blower. Long lines of flatcars laden with iron pit-props and cedar timber stretched behind the engine into the grey mist.

At the center of the train sat a single enclosed passenger coach: an old wooden carriage with narrow slit-windows, reinforced at the corners with bolted angle-iron to withstand rockfalls and beast attacks along the mountain passes.

The squad climbed the iron steps into the carriage.

The interior smelled of old pitch, coal soot, and damp floorboards. Two rows of split-oak benches ran along the walls. The only heat came from a small cast-iron stove bolted to the center of the floor, its fluted pipe venting through the ceiling.

Anil threw his canvas kit-bag onto a corner bench and sat down, rubbing his palms together over the iron stove. "Sixty miles through the mountains on an iron box. At least there are no proctors trying to split our skulls with stone mauls."

"Do not speak so soon," Tejaswini said, sitting opposite him and laying her sabre across her knees. She unbuckled her leather collar to let the heat reach her throat. "If corpse-beasts have reached the Langford mining shafts, they are not strays. A Vetala pack requires a nesting mound to sustain its animus."

Chaitanya stood by the narrow window, wiping a circle of soot from the glass with his glove.

Outside, the locomotive’s brass whistle screamed: a long, mournful wail that tore through the mountain valley, scattering a flock of black ravens from the spruce trees along the track.

The heavy drive-rods clanked. With a violent shudder that rattled every tooth in their jaws, the iron train leaned into the grade, its steel wheels biting into the frozen rails as it began its descent into the dark, uncharted valleys of the south.
